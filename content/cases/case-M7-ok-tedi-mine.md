## The Crisis

The Ok Tedi copper and gold mine in the remote Western Province of Papua New Guinea began operations in 1984 under a joint venture led by BHP, with Amoco and the PNG government as partners. Almost immediately, the project encountered its foundational environmental problem: a tailings storage dam failure in 1984 forced the abandonment of the planned containment system before the mine was fully operational. Rather than halt production to construct an alternative solution, the operating consortium — with PNG government acquiescence — began disposing of mine tailings directly into the Ok Tedi River, a tributary of the Fly River system. This was framed as a temporary, emergency measure. It continued for nearly 30 years.

By the 1990s, approximately 80,000 tonnes of mine waste was being discharged into the river system every single day. The cumulative environmental impact was catastrophic: over 1,000 kilometres of the Ok Tedi and Fly River systems were contaminated with heavy metals; sediment buildup caused widespread riverbank die-off, destroying the forest and agricultural systems that indigenous Yonggom, Awin, and Fly River communities depended on for subsistence. An estimated 50,000 people across dozens of villages experienced direct harm to their livelihoods, food systems, and cultural relationship to the river. BHP's own internal environmental studies, later disclosed, confirmed the company understood the scale of damage years before it was publicly acknowledged.

In 1994, a group of landowners — represented by Melbourne law firm Slater & Gordon — filed a class action lawsuit in the Australian state of Victoria, seeking compensation for environmental and cultural harm. BHP's response was not remediation or settlement; it was suppression. The company lobbied the PNG government to enact legislation — the Eighth Supplemental Agreement — that made it illegal for PNG citizens to pursue overseas lawsuits against Ok Tedi Mining Limited. This legislative manoeuvre, when reported in the Australian and international press, transformed a manageable environmental liability into a case study in corporate abuse of political power. The legislation was deeply controversial within Papua New Guinea and internationally condemned by human rights and environmental NGOs.

A settlement was eventually reached in 1996, with BHP paying approximately $28 million USD to affected communities and establishing an environmental trust fund. However, the company continued tailings disposal and the fundamental environmental damage continued. BHP divested its stake in 2002, issuing a statement that the mine was "not compatible with our environmental values" — a line widely ridiculed as the company exiting a mess of its own making with minimal accountability. The mine continued operating under PNG government and landowner trust control, using the same river disposal practices, for years thereafter.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

BHP's communications strategy across the arc of the Ok Tedi crisis failed at virtually every level and in every phase. The initial framing of river disposal as "temporary" established a pattern of systematic misrepresentation that persisted for over a decade. There was no credible public commitment to find an alternative solution, no transparent disclosure of the environmental monitoring data the company possessed, and no genuine engagement with the communities who were visibly losing their ability to fish, farm, and live beside the river.

The lobbying campaign to enact the Eighth Supplemental Agreement was the most damaging communications event of the entire crisis. When exposed, it confirmed for global audiences — NGOs, institutional investors, journalists, and the Australian public — that BHP was willing to use its relationship with a developing-country government to extinguish the legal rights of indigenous people rather than accept accountability. No amount of subsequent charitable commitment or environmental trust funding could neutralize the reputational impact of that specific act. The story migrated from specialist environmental reporting to mainstream international business and human rights coverage, reaching audiences with no prior knowledge of Papua New Guinea.

BHP's eventual 2002 exit statement — describing the mine as incompatible with its "environmental values" — was received as corporate hypocrisy at its most brazen, given that BHP had operated and defended the mine for 18 years. The exit framing served the company's internal ESG positioning but alienated communities, NGOs, and journalists who had tracked the case for years.

### What Partially Worked / What Worked

The 1996 settlement, while inadequate relative to the scale of harm and extracted through litigation rather than voluntary action, did represent a formal acknowledgment of community harm and established a compensatory mechanism and environmental trust. It halted the immediate legal escalation in Australian courts. BHP's eventual public acknowledgment that the mine represented an irreconcilable conflict with responsible environmental practice — even if the timing and framing were self-serving — contributed over the long term to the global mining industry's evolving acknowledgment that river tailings disposal cannot be treated as an acceptable long-term operating model.

## Outcome & Legacy

- The Ok Tedi case became one of the defining global cases in corporate liability for environmental harm to indigenous communities and remains a standard reference in international environmental law.
- BHP's lobbying of the PNG government to suppress community legal rights was cited as a precedent case in subsequent UN discussions on corporate accountability and the development of the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (Ruggie Principles, 2011).
- The Eighth Supplemental Agreement was eventually amended in 2001 to restore landowners' legal rights, in part due to sustained NGO and international pressure.
- River system contamination in the Ok Tedi and Fly catchments persisted decades after the initial crisis and continued to affect fish populations, riverbank vegetation, and community food security well into the 2010s.
- The case directly influenced the development of ICMM environmental management standards and reinforced the unacceptability of riverine tailings disposal as an industry norm.
- BHP's ESG reputation suffered measurably through the 1990s and early 2000s; the Ok Tedi legacy is regularly cited in case studies examining the gap between stated corporate values and operational practice.

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. "Temporary" emergency measures that continue for decades are not temporary — they are policy. Any communications framing that characterizes ongoing harmful practice as provisional must be backed by a credible, time-bound alternative plan or it becomes a compounding liability.
2. Attempting to extinguish community legal rights through government lobbying is not a crisis management tool — it is an escalation mechanism that converts a legal dispute into a human rights and governance crisis.
3. Internal environmental data that contradicts public statements is a time bomb. When it surfaces — and in litigation, it will — it retroactively reframes every prior statement as knowing deception rather than error.
4. An exit from an asset cannot close a reputational crisis if the exit statement fails to accept proportionate accountability. "Not compatible with our values" language, when used after 18 years of operation, reads as self-serving revisionism.
5. Community legal rights are not negotiable assets in a political transaction. The perception that a mining company purchased legislation from a developing-country government to silence indigenous plaintiffs will outlast any financial settlement.
6. NGO sustained campaigning compounds over time. The Ok Tedi case shows how a single mining operation can drive global policy change on indigenous rights and corporate accountability when communities find effective advocacy partners.
7. Social license cannot be retroactively constructed. The absence of any genuine community engagement architecture from 1984 onward meant there was no trust relationship to draw on when crisis struck.

## Latin America Application

| Mistake/Lesson | LATAM Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| River contamination framed as temporary without alternative plan | LATAM river systems (Amazon tributaries, Andean watersheds) serve as drinking and subsistence sources for large indigenous populations; "temporary" contamination framing triggers ILO 169 violations and national-level political crises | Never communicate an emergency environmental measure without simultaneously publishing a time-bound alternative plan and an independent monitoring commitment |
| Lobbying government to suppress community legal rights | In Peru, Colombia, and Argentina, companies perceived to be engineering political solutions to legal accountability face accelerated social mobilization and international NGO coalitions | Any government engagement on regulatory matters should be disclosed publicly; companies should explicitly affirm community legal access rights in all CSR and community relations communications |
| Concealment of internal environmental assessments | LATAM operations frequently conduct environmental impact assessments under regulatory requirement; selective disclosure triggers both regulatory sanction and media amplification when documents surface | Establish a policy of voluntary disclosure of environmental monitoring data to community liaison committees; what is shared proactively cannot be weaponized against the company |
| Exit without accountability | BHP's divestment exit framing is a cautionary tale for LATAM operators considering asset sales in communities with unresolved environmental or social claims | Any asset sale in a contested community context should include a specific, funded remediation and community transition plan communicated before the transaction closes |
