## The Crisis

Johnson & Johnson's Baby Powder, a cornstarch and talc-based product marketed to mothers and infants for over a century, became the center of one of the most consequential consumer product safety crises in U.S. history. In December 2018, Reuters published an investigation revealing that J&J had known since at least 1971 that its raw talc sometimes tested positive for asbestos contamination — and had never disclosed this to regulators or consumers. The Reuters report was based on thousands of pages of internal company documents, including memos, scientific reports, and correspondence with talc suppliers.

The internal documents showed a pattern of deliberate risk management rather than transparent safety disclosure. J&J scientists and executives were aware that asbestos fibers — a known carcinogen linked to mesothelioma and ovarian cancer — had been detected in talc samples from its supplier mines. Internal communications showed that when tests came back positive, the company worked to identify testing methodologies that would produce negative results, and challenged or discredited external scientists who published findings linking talc to cancer. J&J maintained publicly that its products were "safe and asbestos-free" throughout this period.

By the time of the Reuters investigation, J&J was already defending approximately 11,700 lawsuits from women who alleged that talc-based baby powder had caused ovarian cancer. The revelation of the internal documents fundamentally altered the litigation landscape. In May 2020, J&J announced it was withdrawing talc-based baby powder from U.S. and Canadian markets, citing — not safety concerns — but "misinformation" around the safety of the product and supply disruptions related to the COVID-19 pandemic. The company maintained the product was safe while withdrawing it. J&J continued selling talc-based baby powder in Latin America, Asia, and other developing markets until August 2022.

The dual-market strategy — withdrawing from wealthy, litigious markets while continuing to sell in developing markets with weaker consumer protection regimes — attracted intense media and NGO scrutiny and was widely characterized as a deliberate exploitation of regulatory asymmetry. J&J ultimately discontinued all talc-based baby powder globally in August 2022.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

CEO Alex Gorsky's immediate response to the December 2018 Reuters investigation was a categorical failure. In a video statement, Gorsky called the Reuters reporting "irresponsible" and stated unequivocally that J&J's products were "safe." The company did not acknowledge the internal documents revealed by Reuters or address the substance of the asbestos testing findings. This response — attacking the journalism without engaging the evidence — immediately positioned J&J adversarially against a well-documented investigation and provided no path to credibility restoration.

The May 2020 withdrawal announcement was strategically incoherent. J&J used "misinformation" and "supply chain disruption" as the stated rationale for withdrawing a product from U.S. and Canadian markets. By simultaneously maintaining that the product was safe while withdrawing it, J&J created a permanent communications contradiction. Any future claim that the withdrawal was safety-motivated was foreclosed; any claim that it was not safety-motivated was implausible. The company occupied no defensible position.

The dual-market strategy was the most damaging communications failure. J&J's decision to continue selling talc-based baby powder in Latin America, Asia, and other developing markets after the U.S. and Canadian withdrawal was reported by Reuters, Bloomberg, and AP. The explicit two-tier standard — protecting consumers in wealthy countries while continuing to sell a potentially contaminated product in developing markets — generated outrage from NGOs, health advocates, and foreign governments. It also directly undermined any claim of company-wide commitment to consumer safety.

J&J's attempt to use a divisional bankruptcy strategy — creating a subsidiary called LTL Management and filing for Chapter 11 to consolidate and cap talc liability — was struck down twice by federal appeals courts (2022 and 2023) on the grounds that LTL was not in genuine financial distress. The repeated failures of the bankruptcy strategy kept the litigation narrative active for additional years and produced a further round of damaging headlines each time a court rejected the approach.

### What Partially Worked

J&J's legal team executed an effective early litigation containment strategy in the years before the Reuters investigation, achieving settlements in individual cases without creating broad legal precedent. The company also maintained strong financial performance throughout the crisis, suggesting that investor confidence was partially sustained by J&J's diversification across pharmaceuticals, medical devices, and consumer products. However, these are legal and financial achievements rather than communications successes, and they ultimately did not prevent the reputational collapse.

## Outcome & Legacy

- J&J's total talc litigation liability is estimated at over $6.5 billion in settlements and verdicts.
- Talc-based baby powder was globally discontinued in August 2022, replaced with a cornstarch formula.
- The "Texas Two-Step" bankruptcy strategy — using a divisional subsidiary to contain mass tort liability — was struck down twice by the Third Circuit Court of Appeals and became a significant legal precedent limiting similar maneuvers.
- CEO Alex Gorsky retired in 2022; his handling of the talc crisis was widely cited in coverage of his tenure.
- The dual-market withdrawal received specific attention from the WHO and consumer advocacy organizations in multiple LatAm countries.
- The case has become a primary reference for discussions of corporate two-tier safety standards in global health communications.
- J&J's consumer division (now Kenvue, spun off in 2023) carries reputational legacy effects that affected the spinoff's public market reception.

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Calling accurate investigative journalism "irresponsible" without addressing the substance is not a communications strategy.** J&J's "irresponsible" framing of the Reuters investigation had no path to credibility — the documents cited were real, and every subsequent discovery release confirmed them. This response extended the adversarial narrative for years.
2. **Withdrawing a product while claiming it is safe creates a permanent contradiction.** The decision to frame the U.S./Canada withdrawal around "misinformation" rather than precautionary action was designed to limit liability but produced maximum communications damage. There is no defensible position in simultaneously claiming safety and withdrawing a product from major markets.
3. **Dual-market safety standards are not survivable in the global media environment.** The decision to continue selling in developing markets after the U.S. and Canadian withdrawal was reported by international media within weeks and provided a global "two-tier" narrative that proved more damaging than the original product safety story.
4. **Creative legal strategies to limit mass tort liability generate their own communications crises.** Each round of bankruptcy strategy coverage redirected media attention to J&J's attempts to avoid accountability rather than to any positive corporate action. Legal strategy and communications strategy must be aligned.
5. **Fifty years of prior knowledge cannot be managed through crisis communication.** No communications response is adequate to a documented half-century of internal knowledge suppression. The only viable path at the time of the Reuters investigation was radical transparency — the option J&J did not take.
6. **Product withdrawal timing signals intent.** The three-year gap between U.S./Canada withdrawal (May 2020) and global withdrawal (August 2022) was interpreted — correctly — as a deliberate decision to continue monetizing a questioned product in markets with less regulatory and litigation exposure. The timing became central to the human rights narrative.
7. **CEO personal accountability statements require factual precision.** Gorsky's "irresponsible" characterization of Reuters was specific enough to be definitively disproven but not substantive enough to engage the actual evidence. An effective CEO response in this situation requires acknowledging what the documents show and explaining what the company will do about it.
