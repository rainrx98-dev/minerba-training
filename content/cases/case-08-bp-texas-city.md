## The Crisis

On March 23, 2005, at 1:20 PM, an explosion and fire tore through the isomerization unit at BP's Texas City refinery — at the time the third-largest refinery in the United States. A raffinate splitter tower was overfilled during a startup procedure, releasing a flammable hydrocarbon cloud that ignited. Fifteen contract workers were killed, all of them in temporary trailers positioned near the unit in violation of BP's own siting guidelines. Another 180 people were injured, and the explosion was felt miles away. It remains one of the deadliest industrial accidents in US history.

The disaster was not unforeseeable. A 2004 internal BP audit — part of a process that reviewed all five of BP's US refineries — had identified serious safety deficiencies at Texas City. The audit findings were documented but not acted upon at the pace or scale required. The refinery had experienced 23 worker fatalities in the preceding 30 years. In the months before the explosion, workers had raised concerns about unsafe startup procedures. A report later noted that cost-cutting pressure had resulted in the deferral of critical maintenance and the underfunding of safety infrastructure across BP's US downstream operations.

The immediate operational failure involved a series of procedural errors during startup: a level indicator was malfunctioning, alarms were bypassed, and supervisory oversight was inadequate. But the Baker Panel — an independent review board chaired by former Secretary of State James Baker and commissioned by BP itself — concluded in January 2007 that the root cause was not individual error but a systemic failure of BP's process safety culture. BP corporate leadership, the Panel found, had focused on personal safety metrics (slips, trips, falls) while neglecting the management of catastrophic process safety risk.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

**CEO Absence:** CEO John Browne was in London when the explosion occurred. Rather than travelling immediately to Texas City, BP sent a Vice President to the site. Browne's absence in the critical first 72 hours left BP without an authoritative, empathetic voice and allowed media and regulators to frame the story entirely around corporate negligence. The contrast with what a CEO's physical presence signals — accountability, urgency, compassion — was noticed and reported.

**Investigation-First Posture:** BP's early public communications focused almost entirely on the commencement of investigations and the cooperation of the company with authorities. Statements about the victims and their families were brief, formulaic, and came across as secondary to legal and procedural concerns. Families of workers who died reported feeling that the company's priorities were not with them.

**Deflection to Contractors:** Several early communications noted that the deceased were contract workers employed by third-party firms. While technically accurate, this framing was perceived — and widely reported — as a distancing maneuver: BP appeared to be suggesting that the deaths were, in some sense, someone else's responsibility. It created immediate backlash among labor advocates, unions, and the congressional delegations of the affected states.

**Failure to Acknowledge Prior Warnings:** BP did not proactively disclose the existence of the 2004 safety audit findings in its initial communications. When investigative reporters and regulators uncovered the audit, the gap between what BP knew and what it had said publicly became a major story in its own right — compounding reputational damage beyond the disaster itself.

**Silence on Safety Culture:** No BP executive publicly acknowledged systemic safety culture failure in the immediate aftermath. The Baker Panel report, issued nearly two years later, confirmed what BP's own internal data had shown. The two-year silence between the explosion and that acknowledgment maintained a state of ongoing credibility erosion.

### What Partially Worked

**Baker Panel Commission:** BP's decision to commission the independent Baker Panel — and to ultimately publish its findings — represented a degree of institutional accountability unusual for a company in crisis. The Panel's report was substantive, named systemic failures clearly, and gave regulators, journalists, and the public a credible basis for understanding what happened. This did not recover BP's reputation but did demonstrate a willingness to submit to independent scrutiny.

**Settlement Engagement:** BP eventually settled with the families of the deceased and injured workers, and engaged cooperatively with OSHA's investigation process. The $21.3M OSHA fine — the largest in OSHA history at the time — was not contested in a manner that prolonged public hostility.

## Outcome & Legacy

- **15 workers killed**, 180 injured in the single deadliest US industrial accident of that decade
- **OSHA fined BP $21.3M** — the largest safety fine in OSHA history at the time of issuance
- **EPA fined BP $50M** for Clean Air Act violations related to the explosion
- **Total costs exceeded $1.5B**, including settlements, fines, and remediation
- The Baker Panel identified BP's failure to maintain a strong process safety culture as the systemic cause — naming a distinction between personal safety and process safety that is now foundational in industrial safety management
- The same safety culture failures documented by the Baker Panel — deferred maintenance, cost pressure overriding risk management, inadequate management of catastrophic process risk — were independently confirmed as contributing factors in the 2010 Deepwater Horizon explosion, which killed 11 workers and triggered the largest marine oil spill in US history
- BP's Texas City case accelerated the development of the US Chemical Safety Board's authority and investigative capacity

## Key PR Lessons

1. **CEO physical presence is non-negotiable within 24 hours of a mass casualty event.** Sending a VP signals that the lives lost are not worth the CEO's time.
2. **Investigation-first language reads as victim-last priorities.** Empathy and accountability must precede procedural language in every public statement.
3. **Contractor framing as distancing is immediately transparent.** If your infrastructure, your permits, your site: own it fully.
4. **Known risks that were not acted on become the story.** Proactive disclosure of prior warnings — on your terms — is vastly preferable to their discovery by journalists.
5. **Two-year gaps between disaster and accountability are not silence — they are active reputational damage.** Every month without acknowledgment is a month during which critics fill the void.
6. **Personal safety metrics are not process safety.** Communications that cite low injury rates during a catastrophic failure are not only irrelevant — they are insulting to victims.
7. **The precursor disaster is the last chance.** Texas City was a documented precursor to Deepwater Horizon. Organizations that fail to communicate lessons internally — and to act on them — create the conditions for the next, larger crisis.

## Latin America Application

| BP Mistake | LATAM Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| CEO absent; VP dispatched to site | In Mexico, Colombia, and Brazil, a CEO's physical absence at a mass casualty event triggers immediate political escalation — congressional summons, ministerial intervention, community blockades | Pre-designate a CEO travel protocol: mass casualty events require C-suite presence within 24 hours regardless of geography |
| Contractor framing deflects responsibility | Subcontracting is pervasive in LatAm energy; "they were contractors" is recognized by unions and government as a liability evasion formula and accelerates regulatory hostility | Communications must claim full operational responsibility for all site personnel regardless of employment structure |
| Prior audit findings not disclosed | In jurisdictions with active attorney general offices (Brazil, Colombia, Peru), withheld safety records become criminal evidence — not just PR liabilities | Establish a proactive safety disclosure protocol: known risks that were unaddressed must be communicated to regulators before, not after, an incident |
| Safety culture failure not acknowledged for two years | LatAm media cycles are faster; two years of silence produces sustained congressional and NGO pressure campaigns, license challenges, and community opposition | Commission independent safety culture reviews after serious incidents and publish findings within 12 months |
| Pattern of failures not connected across operations | BP's same culture failure produced Texas City and Deepwater Horizon; multi-country operators in LatAm face cross-border regulatory coordination (IIRSA, regional environmental bodies) | Treat each serious incident as a system-level signal; conduct cross-country operational audits and report findings to regional regulators proactively |
