## The Crisis

On March 24, 1989, at 12:04 AM, the oil tanker *Exxon Valdez* struck Bligh Reef in Prince William Sound, Alaska. Within six hours, approximately 10.9 million gallons of crude oil had poured into one of America's most pristine marine ecosystems. The spill ultimately contaminated over 1,300 miles of coastline, killed an estimated 250,000 seabirds, 2,800 sea otters, 300 harbour seals, 250 bald eagles, and up to 22 killer whales. Local fishing communities were devastated. The remote location — accessible only by helicopter or boat — made early response nearly impossible.

Contributing factors included:
- The ship was understaffed (20 crew vs. recommended 33), with workers averaging 140 hours of overtime per month
- The captain had a prior drunk driving record and was allegedly intoxicated; a third mate was at the helm
- Exxon's contingency plan promised spill containment within five hours — a figure that had never been tested
- Response equipment was not pre-positioned; for 35 hours after the spill, no meaningful containment began

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

**CEO Absence (Days 1–10):** CEO Lawrence Rawl issued no public statement for ten days. When he finally spoke, it was via a newspaper letter focused on logistics — no apology, no empathy. He never visited the spill site. In the vacuum, critics, local officials, and journalists defined the narrative entirely.

**Scapegoating:** Exxon's early communications implied that the captain's alleged intoxication was the primary cause. This strategy backfired: the captain's prior drunk driving record raised questions about Exxon's own hiring and oversight standards. Crew members testified about chronic fatigue driven by Exxon cost-cutting. The scapegoat pointed straight back at the corporation.

**Fragmented Spokespersons:** With no crisis communications plan and no PR director in the company structure, multiple Exxon employees spoke to media independently — Don Cornet (Alaska coordinator), Frank Iarossi (Exxon Shipping president), David Parish (worldwide spokesperson). Their statements contradicted each other on timelines, spill size, and cleanup progress. Contradiction became the story.

**Iarossi's Disastrous Press Conference:** On Day 6, the president of Exxon Shipping held a press conference without PR briefing and was described as "verbally slaughtered" by reporters. Every answer generated new damaging headlines.

**Untested Crisis Plan:** The publicly stated five-hour containment promise was exposed as fiction. The gap between promise and observable reality destroyed all remaining credibility in a single news cycle.

**Legal-First Posture:** Public communications emphasized liability technicalities rather than human and ecological impact. Audiences and journalists recognized this as deflection. It raised suspicion of systemic cover-up.

**Contradictory Statements:** On March 27, Rawl denied knowledge of cuts to the spill response team. Two days later, Exxon stated environmental effects would be "larger than anticipated." On March 29, they admitted they would be unable to contain the spill. Each new statement made the previous one look like a lie.

### What Partially Worked

**On-Ground Community Campaign:** Don Cornet in Alaska hired PR and advertising firms to develop a targeted recovery campaign for three devastated sectors: tourism, seafood, and wildlife rescue. This campaign reached approximately 50 million Americans. Exxon co-branded tourism promotion with Alaska's governor, subordinating Exxon's visibility to state leadership — a tactically sound move. Animal rescue centers, visibly funded by Exxon, gave media a "recovery story" to tell alongside the disaster.

**Full-Page Newspaper Ads:** Exxon published full-page ads expressing concern and committing to cleanup. These reached significant audiences but arrived too late and lacked empathy language. They competed against live broadcast imagery of oil-covered wildlife and could not win.

**Financial Resources:** Exxon's scale allowed it to fund cleanup, settlements, and promotional efforts simultaneously. The eventual $2B+ cleanup effort was real, even if the communications around it were disastrous.

## Outcome & Legacy

- **$2B+** spent on cleanup operations
- **$1B+** in fines, penalties, and legal settlements
- Exxon brand became synonymous with corporate environmental negligence globally
- Triggered the **Oil Pollution Act of 1990**, mandating stronger spill prevention, double-hull tankers, and response capability requirements
- Rawl's management style became a textbook example of CEO communication failure
- Case remains the primary reference point in corporate crisis communications training 35+ years later

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. **The golden window is real.** The first 24–48 hours determine who controls the narrative. Silence cedes that control entirely to critics.
2. **CEO absence is not neutral.** Every hour without executive-level communication is interpreted as guilt or indifference.
3. **Scapegoating invites scrutiny of the scapegoater.** Any blame transfer must be airtight — if the deflected blame can bounce back, it lands with greater force.
4. **One voice, one message.** Fragmented spokespersons guarantee contradictions. Contradictions become the story.
5. **Crisis plans must be tested.** An untested plan that fails publicly is worse than having no plan — it proves deliberate negligence.
6. **Legal-first language is a PR liability.** Liability framing is read as evasion. Empathy must lead; legal protection follows.
7. **Community-centred action builds more trust than corporate messaging.** The tourism and wildlife recovery campaign worked precisely because it served affected communities, not Exxon's brand.

## Latin America Application

| Exxon Mistake | Latin America Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| No CEO statement for 10 days | Social/political pressure cycles move faster; Day 3 brings congressional hearings and community blockades | Pre-record bilingual (ES/PT) CEO holding statements; designate spokesperson before operations begin |
| Scapegoating individual | Deflection onto workers is weaponized by unions and left-leaning governments | Lead with systemic responsibility and concrete remediation when incidents occur |
| Fragmented spokespersons | Multi-country operations multiply contradictions across borders instantly | Build unified regional communications command: one crisis lead per country, one regional coordinator |
| Print ads lost to live broadcast imagery | WhatsApp, local radio, and indigenous community social media distribute video before any corporate response | Pre-build community engagement channels; respond in community channels before national media |
| No PR function in corporate structure | Energy firms in LatAm routinely treat communications as aftermarket — it is operationally fatal | Hire in-country Corporate Affairs directors with local relationships before first operations begin |
