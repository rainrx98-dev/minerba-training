## The Crisis

Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV) is a live attenuated dengue fever vaccine developed by Sanofi Pasteur over more than two decades. In 2015 and 2016, it was approved in several countries with high dengue burden, including the Philippines, Brazil, El Salvador, Costa Rica, Paraguay, Peru, Indonesia, Thailand, and Singapore. The Philippine government under President Benigno Aquino III launched an ambitious school-based mass vaccination program in 2016, vaccinating approximately 830,000 children in public schools in highly dengue-affected regions.

Dengue fever is caused by four serotypes of the dengue virus (DENV1–4). A critical characteristic of dengue is that a second infection with a different serotype carries a significantly higher risk of severe dengue — a phenomenon called antibody-dependent enhancement. When Dengvaxia was approved, the safety and efficacy data showed positive results across the population studied. However, a critical subset was not adequately characterized: children who had never been previously infected with dengue (seronegative individuals). In seronegative recipients, Dengvaxia essentially functions as a first dengue infection — meaning a subsequent natural infection carries the elevated risk of severe dengue that second infections produce.

In November 2017, Sanofi published new long-term analysis from its clinical trial data and issued a public statement acknowledging that Dengvaxia could increase the risk of severe dengue in individuals who had not been previously infected when vaccinated. This was a significant safety reversal from the original approval communications. The statement was scientifically accurate and represented responsible long-term post-market pharmacovigilance — Sanofi disclosed what the data showed as soon as the analysis was complete.

However, the manner in which the disclosure was made was catastrophically inadequate from a public health communications standpoint. Sanofi released the statement without first briefing the Philippine Department of Health with a clear protocol for communicating to parents, managing follow-up care for vaccinated children, or addressing the public health messaging vacuum that would immediately follow. The company had 830,000 vaccinated children in the Philippines with no systematic serostatus data and no established plan for how parents should respond.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

The November 2017 disclosure was a unilateral corporate action released without coordination with Philippine health authorities, WHO, or regional health bodies. There was no "what to do now" messaging prepared for the 830,000 families whose children had been vaccinated. Parents had no way to determine whether their children had been seronegative at the time of vaccination, no guidance on monitoring for severe dengue symptoms, and no accessible medical consultation pathway. The information vacuum was immediately filled by fear, speculation, and political amplification.

The political environment in the Philippines under President Rodrigo Duterte was particularly hostile to technocratic messaging. The government immediately halted the vaccination program and announced a criminal investigation into the officials who had approved and procured the vaccine, including Health Secretary Paulyn Jean Rosell-Ubial, who resigned. Duterte's communications on the issue were inflammatory and anti-scientific: he described Dengvaxia as a "poisonous" substance and publicly suggested children had been killed. Several children's deaths were formally investigated for possible links to the vaccine, and while scientific consensus held that most were not causally linked, the absence of a credible counter-narrative allowed the "Dengvaxia killed children" frame to dominate public discourse in the Philippines.

France's national medicines agency (ANSM) suspended the use of Dengvaxia in France pending review. WHO updated its guidance to recommend pre-vaccination screening to identify seronegative individuals — a recommendation that effectively limited the vaccine's public health utility in settings without accessible serology testing infrastructure. Sanofi's regulatory position was weakened in multiple markets simultaneously.

The most severe long-term consequence was the collapse of vaccine confidence in the Philippines. Studies published in the Lancet and other journals documented that general childhood vaccine coverage — for measles, polio, and other diseases — declined significantly in the Philippines following the Dengvaxia crisis. This "vaccine hesitancy spillover" culminated in a 2019 measles outbreak that infected over 47,000 people and killed approximately 630, primarily children. The measles deaths had no causal connection to the dengue vaccine, but the erosion of general vaccine trust created the coverage gap that enabled the outbreak.

### What Partially Worked

Sanofi's underlying decision to disclose the new safety data proactively and promptly was scientifically and ethically correct. The company did not suppress the long-term trial data when it became unfavorable — it published and communicated it. In a different context, with adequate preparation of authorities and parent communications, this disclosure could have been managed as an example of responsible post-market safety monitoring. The scientific conduct was defensible; the communications execution was not.

## Outcome & Legacy

- Sanofi's Dengvaxia was effectively removed from the Philippine market and its global regulatory standing significantly diminished.
- WHO guidance now requires pre-vaccination serology screening for Dengvaxia, limiting its utility in low-resource settings that cannot afford widespread serology testing.
- Philippine health officials faced criminal charges in connection with the vaccination program procurement and approval.
- The measles outbreak of 2019 is cited in public health literature as a consequence of vaccine hesitancy spillover triggered by the Dengvaxia crisis.
- The case is now foundational in global health communications education on the concept of "vaccine confidence contagion" — the spillover of distrust from one vaccine to the broader immunization program.
- Sanofi's dengue vaccine program, representing decades and hundreds of millions of dollars of R&D investment, was commercially devastated.
- The case influenced WHO's global guidelines for vaccine crisis communications and the importance of coordinated government-manufacturer messaging protocols.

## Key PR Lessons

1. **A scientifically accurate safety disclosure can still be a communications catastrophe if it is not accompanied by a parent/patient action plan.** Sanofi's November 2017 statement was factually correct and ethically obligatory — but it generated maximum fear because it told families what the risk was without telling them what to do about it.
2. **Vaccine safety disclosures must be coordinated with health authorities before public release.** The unilateral release of the November 2017 statement was the critical strategic error. A simultaneous release with a government-prepared response protocol and parent communication campaign would have been materially different in outcome.
3. **Vaccine hesitancy spillover is a predictable and documented risk.** When one vaccine is associated with harm in the public mind, distrust of other vaccines rises. This effect was documented before the Dengvaxia crisis and Sanofi's communications strategy should have explicitly addressed the risk to broader vaccination confidence.
4. **Political context determines how accurate information is received.** The Philippines under Duterte was a high-volatility communications environment. Sanofi's failure to prepare for how the Duterte government would handle the disclosure — and to work with moderating voices in advance — left the narrative entirely in hostile hands.
5. **Post-approval pharmacovigilance communications must be built into the product launch plan.** For a vaccine administered to children in a mass public program, the discovery of a safety signal affecting a subset of recipients is a foreseeable event that should have a pre-planned communications protocol.
6. **"We disclosed as required" is not a sufficient defense when the method of disclosure causes independent harm.** The downstream consequence of Sanofi's disclosure method — a measles outbreak that killed hundreds of children — makes the technical compliance with pharmacovigilance obligations insufficient as a moral or reputational position.
7. **In high-trust products (vaccines, pediatric medicine), the social license is both fragile and collectively shared.** A company's handling of safety information does not only affect its own product — it affects the entire immunization ecosystem. This collective responsibility requires higher standards of communications coordination than other product categories.
