## The Crisis

Yanacocha, located in the Cajamarca highlands of northern Peru, is the largest gold mine in Latin America. It operates as a joint venture: Newmont Mining Corporation holds 51.35%, Compañía de Minas Buenaventura holds 43.65%, and the International Finance Corporation (IFC) holds 5%. When operations began in 1993, Yanacocha was presented as an economic transformation story for one of Peru's most impoverished regions. By 2004, it had become the defining Latin American case study in social license destruction — a failure built from accumulated grievance, cultural insensitivity, and a corporate communications posture that persistently dismissed community concerns as politically motivated.

The precipitating crisis of record is the 2004 community uprising against the proposed Quilish expansion, but the conditions that made that uprising inevitable were created four years earlier. On June 2, 2000, a truck contracted by Minera Yanacocha spilled approximately 151 kilograms of liquid mercury near the community of Choropampa on the Pan-American Highway. Mercury contaminated roadsides and water sources; over 1,000 community members — including children — reported symptoms of mercury poisoning. The company's response was slow, inadequate, and contested at every stage. Medical assessments commissioned by the company produced findings that downplayed exposure levels, while independent assessments reached more alarming conclusions. Compensation negotiations were conducted in ways that affected families described as coercive — families were pressured to sign away future legal rights in exchange for modest, immediate payments. By 2004, the mercury spill litigation was still active, the affected families were still uncompensated to their satisfaction, and community trust in Newmont and Buenaventura in the Cajamarca region had been destroyed.

In this context, Newmont announced plans to explore the Quilish Hill area adjacent to the existing Yanacocha operations. Quilish is not only a significant source of fresh water for Cajamarca city — approximately 300,000 people — but also holds deep cultural and spiritual significance for indigenous and campesino communities in the region. Community leaders had been raising concerns about Quilish for years before the 2004 crisis. In late September 2004, after months of escalating tension, mass protests erupted. Thousands of Cajamarca residents and rural communities blockaded the main roads to the mine. At peak mobilisation, an estimated 10,000 people were involved. The regional government supported the protest. Police deployed to maintain access were vastly outnumbered and unable to restore road access.

Newmont and Buenaventura's CEO Roque Benavides issued public statements characterising the opposition as the work of political agitators and anti-development activists — a framing that misread the social reality of Cajamarca and infuriated communities. After three days of conflict and road blockades, Newmont announced the "suspension" of Quilish exploration activities. The announcement was widely perceived as an unconditional capitulation driven by force rather than dialogue. The suspension did not restore social license; it confirmed its permanent absence in the region.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

The foundational failure was the company's refusal across four years (2000–2004) to genuinely resolve the Choropampa mercury spill with the affected community. The combination of contested medical findings, inadequate compensation, and legal maneuvering to limit liability created a cohort of several thousand community members with direct personal grievances against Minera Yanacocha. These individuals became the most credible voices against the mine when the Quilish crisis emerged — and no amount of corporate communications could neutralize the testimony of families whose children had been sickened by mercury.

The CEO's public characterisation of protest leaders as politically motivated actors was the single most damaging communications decision of the 2004 crisis. In a context where tens of thousands of ordinary Cajamarca residents had joined the protests — farmers, urban residents, teachers, local government officials — the "outside agitator" framing was immediately falsifiable and insulting to the entire community. It confirmed that the company's leadership had not listened to or understood the Cajamarca social landscape and had no credible community intelligence informing their response.

Newmont and Buenaventura's failure to proactively engage with the cultural and spiritual significance of Quilish — treating it as a technical water supply question amenable to hydrology reports rather than as a question of identity and place — was a textbook FPIC failure. The community's relationship to Quilish was publicly known and documented. Choosing to proceed with exploration without addressing this dimension first was a preventable error.

The eventual "suspension" announcement contained no acknowledgment of community concerns as legitimate, no commitment to a process for future engagement, and no pathway for addressing the unresolved Choropampa grievances. It read as a tactical pause, not a genuine change in posture.

### What Partially Worked / What Worked

Newmont's decision not to use significant force to break the road blockades — despite significant economic pressure from production interruption — prevented a confrontation that could have resulted in casualties and a far worse crisis. The relatively rapid operational suspension (within 72 hours of peak protest mobilisation) limited the duration of economic disruption and avoided further escalation. In subsequent years, Newmont invested significantly in community development programs in Cajamarca and commissioned independent reviews of the Choropampa compensation process, though these came too late to restore social license in the Quilish context.

## Outcome & Legacy

- The Quilish expansion has never resumed. The Yanacocha operation continued at existing concessions but Newmont's growth pipeline in Cajamarca was effectively terminated by the social license collapse.
- The Yanacocha case is the most frequently cited Latin American example in the academic and practitioner literature on social license to operate, and is required reading in environmental and resource governance courses globally.
- The IFC — as a 5% shareholder — used the case to develop more rigorous social impact assessment requirements for its extractive industry lending, contributing to the revision of the IFC Performance Standards.
- Newmont published a detailed case study acknowledging communications and community relations failures — one of the few mining majors to publicly document its own social license failure for educational purposes.
- The Choropampa mercury spill compensation process remained contested for over a decade; affected families pursued international arbitration and ongoing legal proceedings.
- The case directly influenced Peru's 2011 Prior Consultation Law (Ley de Consulta Previa, implementing ILO 169), which established mandatory consultation processes for extractive projects affecting indigenous communities.
- Community opposition to subsequent mining projects in Cajamarca (including Newmont's Conga project, blocked in 2011-2012) demonstrated that the social license damage from Yanacocha was regional, not project-specific.

## Key PR Lessons

1. Unresolved historic grievances are the single greatest threat to future project social license. A company cannot manage the communications of a new expansion while leaving the injuries of a prior crisis unhealed. Choropampa made Quilish impossible.
2. The "outside agitator" characterisation of community protest is reliably counterproductive. It signals to the entire community that the company does not see them, and it is almost always demonstrably false in the face of mass participation.
3. Water and sacred sites require a categorically different engagement approach than technical infrastructure decisions. Communities will accept many impositions before they will accept threats to water and ancestral territory. The threshold for conflict is close to absolute.
4. FPIC is not a formality to be satisfied by information disclosure. If communities with historically grounded concerns about a project have not reached genuine agreement, proceeding to permitting is a crisis generation decision.
5. Corporate medical assessments in community contamination cases will never be trusted by affected communities. Independent, community-endorsed medical oversight is the only credible path to resolving health impact disputes.
6. IFC and co-investor ESG standards create accountability mechanisms that mining operators cannot simply ignore. Institutional co-investors with public interest mandates will generate their own investigations and public findings.
7. Social license damage in one project contaminates all future projects in the same region. The Conga blockade a decade later was a direct legacy of Yanacocha — a regional, multigenerational withdrawal of community consent.
