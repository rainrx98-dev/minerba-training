## The Crisis

On March 9, 2018, a magnitude 3.6 seismic event struck beneath the Cadia East Panel Cave underground mine, operated by Newcrest Mining near Orange in central New South Wales, Australia. The seismic event caused a significant cave-in in the underground workings and, separately, triggered a low-level failure at one of the mine's tailings storage facilities (TSF). There were no fatalities. There were no injuries. Both facts were known and communicated within hours.

The operational consequences, however, were substantial. The underground cave-in rendered the Cadia East Panel Cave — one of the world's largest and most productive underground gold-copper mines — inoperable for an extended period. Newcrest's total gold production was materially affected; the company estimated total production losses of approximately $75 million over the period of disruption. For a company whose flagship asset had just experienced a structural failure, the financial pressure to minimise, delay, or manage information was significant. Newcrest chose the opposite approach.

The Cadia case is studied not because the crisis was severe — relative to Brumadinho, Samarco, or Mount Polley, the physical consequences were limited — but because the communications response across every dimension represents a practical model for what good looks like. No deaths, no major environmental breach, competent engineering, and above all: a communications posture that treated every stakeholder — investors, regulators, community, media — as deserving accurate, timely, and direct information from the company's leadership. The contrast with cases where companies have had comparable or lesser physical events and catastrophically mishandled the communications is instructive.

The tailings storage facility failure — while contained and not resulting in any off-site environmental impact — was a particular communications test. Post-Samarco (2015) and post-Mount Polley (2014), any TSF event in the global mining industry carried heightened public sensitivity. Newcrest's decision to immediately confirm, characterise, and publish the TSF event — rather than delay pending full assessment — removed the possibility of a "cover-up" narrative emerging and positioned the company as setting the standard for post-Samarco TSF transparency.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Worked

CEO Sandeep Biswas conducted a transparent investor call within 24 hours of the seismic event. He explicitly acknowledged what was known and, critically, what was not yet known — stating clearly that the full extent of the underground damage was still being assessed and that production impact estimates would be updated as information became available. This combination of immediacy and acknowledged uncertainty was unusual in crisis communications practice, where CEOs typically delay until they can present a complete picture. Biswas' call set a tone of executive accountability that defined all subsequent communications.

The company made proactive briefings to the NSW government and NSW Environment Protection Authority before media queries on the TSF failure. This reversed the standard sequence in which regulators learn of incidents from media rather than directly from operators. The EPA was positioned as a partner in assessment rather than as an adversary or regulator to be managed. The NSW EPA's subsequent finding of no regulatory breach — and the NSW government's public commendation of Newcrest's communications approach — directly validated the strategy.

The decision to delay the company's quarterly production report — which would normally have been published on a standard schedule — until accurate information was available demonstrated a visible, deliberate choice of accuracy over speed. In a market context where any production figure release is scrutinised for material information content, this decision required significant internal governance discipline. The company communicated the reason for the delay explicitly: that publishing inaccurate estimates would be worse than publishing late accurate ones. This reasoning was accepted by the market and by regulators.

Community meetings in Orange were held within 48 hours of the event. These meetings were structured around actual information rather than reassurance. Community questions — including about the TSF failure, the hydrology of the area, and the timeline for operational assessment — were answered with available data and clear commitments to update as more was known. The Orange community's relationship with the Cadia operation had been managed through sustained investment in community relations programs in prior years; the crisis communications built on that foundation.

Newcrest commissioned an independent expert panel to investigate the cause of the seismic event and cave-in. The panel's findings were made public. The company did not contest or manage the findings. This transparency on causal investigation — following the model that had been credited as a partial positive in the Mount Polley Independent Expert Panel process — was accepted as standard by regulators, investors, and community observers.

### What Failed

No significant communications failures were identified in independent post-event assessments. Minor critiques centred on the delay between the seismic event and the first public market disclosure — a gap attributable to the need to confirm factual accuracy — but this was explicitly justified by the accuracy-over-speed rationale and accepted by the ASX and institutional investors. The company's production guidance for the affected period required multiple revisions as the full scope of underground damage became clearer, but each revision was communicated proactively with supporting data.

## Outcome & Legacy

- No fatalities, no injuries, no off-site environmental contamination, no regulatory breach finding — the physical outcome was managed effectively.
- Total production loss estimated at approximately $75 million; reputational and investor relations damage was assessed as minimal, with the stock price stabilising rapidly after the initial disclosure.
- The NSW EPA formally commended Newcrest's communications approach in its post-incident assessment — an unusual public endorsement from a regulator that carries significant credibility value.
- The Cadia case was immediately adopted by several leading mining industry bodies and ESG consultancies as the practitioner benchmark for crisis communications best practice in the post-Samarco, post-Mount Polley environment.
- Newcrest's communications approach was cited in ICMM guidance updates as an example of the CEO-led, stakeholder-first, accuracy-prioritised model that the industry should adopt as standard.
- The case demonstrates that effective crisis communications is substantially a pre-crisis investment: the community trust that allowed Newcrest to hold community meetings within 48 hours of the event was built through years of prior community relations work in Orange, not through crisis response skill alone.
- Newcrest Mining was subsequently acquired by Newmont Corporation in 2023, with the Cadia asset — and its social license — forming a core component of the transaction value.

## Key PR Lessons

1. CEO visibility within 24 hours — with acknowledged uncertainty — is more credible than delayed CEO visibility with false certainty. Acknowledging what you do not yet know builds more trust than projecting confident command of a situation you cannot yet fully assess.
2. Brief regulators before media. The sequence in which a regulator learns of a crisis determines whether they become an adversary or a partner. Regulators briefed directly and early are positioned to confirm containment publicly; regulators surprised by news reports become skeptical investigators.
3. Accuracy over speed is a valid and respected choice when the reason is communicated explicitly. Delaying a quarterly report to ensure accuracy — and telling stakeholders why — is categorically different from delaying to manage information.
4. A TSF event in the post-Samarco environment requires immediate, proactive disclosure regardless of severity. The global sensitivity of the topic means that even a contained, minor TSF event managed transparently is infinitely preferable to a minor TSF event managed defensively or with delay.
5. Community meetings within 48 hours should be the standard for any event affecting a local area. Open Q&A formats — which require genuine information readiness — signal respect for community standing and allow the company to shape the initial narrative rather than respond to speculation.
6. Independent expert investigations commissioned and published without interference serve the company's interests by confirming it has nothing to hide. Resisting or contesting expert findings destroys far more credibility than the findings themselves.
7. Crisis communications is a pre-crisis investment. The speed and effectiveness of Newcrest's Cadia response reflected years of community relations work, established regulator relationships, and investor trust built through routine transparent communications — not just good crisis management on the day.
