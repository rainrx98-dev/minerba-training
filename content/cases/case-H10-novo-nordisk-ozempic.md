## The Crisis

Semaglutide, a GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) receptor agonist developed by Novo Nordisk, is marketed as Ozempic for type 2 diabetes management and as Wegovy for chronic weight management. Both drugs were approved by the FDA — Ozempic in 2017, Wegovy in 2021 — and had demonstrated clinical efficacy for their approved indications. The crisis did not emerge from a safety signal or a scientific failure; it emerged from a collision between pharmaceutical supply capacity, social media-driven demand amplification, and long-standing access equity failures in the pharmaceutical system.

Beginning in 2022 and accelerating through 2023, Ozempic became the subject of an extraordinary social media and celebrity media cycle. Reports of celebrities and wealthy individuals using Ozempic off-label for weight loss — rather than for its approved diabetes indication — spread rapidly through Twitter, TikTok, and mainstream media. The "Ozempic face" discourse (referring to facial fat loss as a side effect of rapid weight reduction) became a widely discussed cultural phenomenon. Telehealth platforms began prescribing semaglutide at scale for weight loss, including through compounding pharmacies offering unapproved formulations. Demand for both Ozempic and Wegovy exceeded Novo Nordisk's manufacturing capacity by a significant margin.

The direct consequence for diabetic patients was severe. Pharmacists across the U.S., UK, Australia, and multiple other countries reported that Ozempic was unavailable or severely backordered. Diabetic patients who had been stable on semaglutide were forced to switch medications, often with worse glycemic control outcomes. Endocrinologists publicly described the situation as a patient safety crisis: their most effective medication for type 2 diabetes was being consumed by off-label weight loss users, leaving their diabetic patients without access. The FDA placed Ozempic on its official drug shortage list.

The access equity dimension was equally acute. Wegovy was launched in the U.S. at a list price of approximately $1,349 per month — a price that made it effectively inaccessible to most Americans without comprehensive insurance coverage, and entirely inaccessible to patients in most developing countries. Novo Nordisk's insulin pricing had already attracted significant scrutiny; the GLP-1 pricing crisis extended the narrative of pharmaceutical companies that prioritized wealthy market profits over patient access. In Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa, and South Asia, neither Ozempic nor Wegovy was available at meaningful scale during the period of peak global demand.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

Novo Nordisk's communications on pricing equity were inadequate throughout the crisis. CEO Lars Fruergaard Jørgensen appeared before the U.S. Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions (HELP) Committee in September 2023, facing questions from Senator Bernie Sanders and other members about why Wegovy cost $1,349 per month in the United States compared to approximately $186 in Germany, $92 in the UK, and $59 in Denmark. Jørgensen's responses were technically accurate but communicatively defensive: he described the complexity of the U.S. drug pricing system, emphasized rebates to insurers and pharmacy benefit managers, and noted Novo Nordisk's patient assistance programs. He did not commit to substantive price reductions for the U.S. market.

The Senate hearing was a significant communications failure not because Jørgensen performed badly in technical terms, but because the company had not prepared a proactive pricing equity narrative before the hearing. The contrast between U.S. and European prices had been publicly documented for months. A company with adequate crisis communications preparation would have arrived at the Senate with a concrete, announced pricing action — rather than a defensive explanation of market complexity.

The global access failure — the near-complete unavailability of semaglutide in most of Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa, and South and Southeast Asia during the period of greatest global attention — received less media coverage than the U.S. shortage but represented a more profound equity failure. Novo Nordisk did not have a credible communications strategy for explaining why the world's most talked-about pharmaceutical product was inaccessible to the majority of the world's diabetes burden, which is concentrated in developing countries.

The social media dimension also exposed a communications gap. Novo Nordisk was not an active participant in the TikTok and Instagram discourse that was directly driving off-label use and supply depletion. The company's silence while compounding pharmacies, telehealth platforms, and influencers drove demand amplification left Novo Nordisk without a meaningful voice in the conversation that was creating its crisis.

### What Partially Worked

Novo Nordisk's communications on supply constraints were relatively transparent. The company acknowledged the shortage publicly, provided regular updates to the FDA on manufacturing capacity timelines, and announced significant capital investment in manufacturing expansion. Jørgensen was accessible to media and investor audiences. The company's investor relations communications were consistent and detailed — Novo Nordisk's stock performance reflected strong investor confidence even as the public narrative was negative. The company announced a manufacturing investment of approximately $6.6 billion in expanded production facilities, with a communications strategy that positioned this as evidence of responsible scaling to meet genuine medical need.

Novo Nordisk also made some progress on access: the company announced tiered pricing in select lower-income country markets and worked with global health organizations on access pathways. These actions were real but insufficient relative to the scale of the access gap and were not communicated with adequate urgency or specificity to meaningfully alter the equity narrative.

## Outcome & Legacy

- Novo Nordisk became the most valuable company in Europe by market capitalization in 2023, briefly surpassing all European peers — demonstrating that the commercial success of GLP-1 drugs was extraordinary even as the access equity crisis persisted.
- The drug shortage for diabetic patients was partially resolved by 2024 as manufacturing capacity increased, but the off-label use market continued to be a significant factor.
- Senator Bernie Sanders's investigation into Novo Nordisk pricing became a major media event and contributed to broader U.S. legislative discussions on pharmaceutical pricing.
- Compounding pharmacy semaglutide — unapproved formulations sold at lower prices — became a significant parallel market, raising patient safety concerns and continuing litigation between Novo Nordisk and compounders.
- The Ozempic crisis significantly elevated pharmaceutical access equity as a mainstream media and political topic, accelerating Congressional attention to drug pricing reform.
- WHO listed semaglutide on its Essential Medicines List in 2023, creating a formal framework for global access advocacy.
- The case is now central to discussions of how pharmaceutical companies should communicate about drug access equity, social media amplification of demand, and the relationship between pricing and social license.

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Supply transparency is necessary but not sufficient — equity transparency is equally essential.** Novo Nordisk communicated its manufacturing constraints clearly and credibly. It failed to communicate a credible equity response to the pricing and access crisis. A complete crisis communications response must address both dimensions.
2. **Prepare a proactive pricing equity narrative before a Senate hearing, not in response to one.** The price differential between the U.S. and European markets was publicly known months before the September 2023 hearing. Arriving at a Congressional hearing without an announced pricing action — after months of adverse coverage — is a communications strategy failure.
3. **Social media-driven demand amplification requires active corporate communications presence, not silence.** Novo Nordisk was essentially absent from the TikTok and social media conversation that was directly driving its own shortage crisis. This allowed telehealth platforms, influencers, and compounders to define the demand narrative entirely.
4. **Being the world's most commercially successful company during an access crisis is a reputational paradox that requires active management.** Novo Nordisk's stock performance and European market cap leadership were regularly juxtaposed in reporting with images of empty pharmacy shelves and diabetic patients without medication. This juxtaposition requires a communications response — not silence.
5. **Tiered pricing and access programs must be communicated as commitments, not concessions.** Novo Nordisk's access programs were real but were perceived as inadequate and reactive. Access equity commitments made before or during a crisis are more credible than those made in response to Senate pressure.
6. **Off-label use by wealthy patients displacing access for approved patient populations is a predictable social license risk for any blockbuster drug.** The dynamic — celebrity off-label use → social media amplification → telehealth scaling → supply depletion for indicated patients — was visible months before it became a crisis. A crisis protocol for this scenario should have been prepared before Wegovy was launched.
7. **Social license can degrade rapidly even when a product is genuinely beneficial.** Semaglutide is a clinically effective medication for diabetes and obesity. Its social license crisis emerged not from safety failures but from access inequity. This demonstrates that benefit alone does not protect social license — equitable access is a prerequisite.
