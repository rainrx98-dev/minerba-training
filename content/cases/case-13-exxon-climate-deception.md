## The Crisis

Beginning in September 2015, investigative reporters at InsideClimate News and the Los Angeles Times published a series of deeply sourced investigations revealing that Exxon (later ExxonMobil) had conducted sophisticated internal research on climate change from the late 1970s onward — and that this research had accurately modeled global warming and its consequences. Internal documents showed that by 1977, Exxon scientists were briefing senior management that burning fossil fuels would cause significant global warming. By 1982, internal memos projected specific warming trajectories that closely matched what later became the scientific consensus.

This internal knowledge existed in direct contradiction to the company's decades-long public posture. From the late 1980s through the mid-2000s, ExxonMobil had been a major funder of climate denial. The company donated approximately $16 million between 1998 and 2005 to organizations that systematically cast doubt on climate science, including the Global Climate Coalition, the George C. Marshall Institute, and dozens of think tanks and academic programs. A 1998 memo drafted by the American Petroleum Institute — in which Exxon was a key participant — outlined a strategic plan to "reposition global warming as theory rather than fact" and to recruit and train scientists willing to dispute the mainstream consensus.

The investigative series — quickly branded "Exxon Knew" by climate advocacy groups — was explosive because it framed ExxonMobil's conduct not merely as aggressive lobbying or motivated corporate speech, but as deliberate institutional deception: the company had known the truth, funded disinformation that contradicted its own findings, and done so for decades. The Columbia School of Journalism partnered with the Los Angeles Times to independently verify the reporting. By early 2016, the framing had shifted from "what did Exxon know?" to "did Exxon commit securities fraud by misrepresenting climate risk to investors?"

In November 2015, New York Attorney General Eric Schneiderman opened a formal investigation under the Martin Act — a powerful New York securities fraud statute — subpoenaing decades of Exxon's internal documents. By 2016, a coalition of more than a dozen state attorneys general, styled "AGs United for Clean Power," had joined or signaled support for similar investigations. The Securities and Exchange Commission also launched a separate inquiry into whether Exxon had accurately disclosed climate-related risks to investors.

---

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

ExxonMobil's communications response was aggressive, legalistic, and strategically counterproductive. CEO Rex Tillerson repeatedly dismissed the attorney general investigations as "politically motivated" and driven by "the green agenda." This framing might have resonated with sympathetic audiences but was damaging with the broader media, institutional investors, and policymakers. Rather than acknowledging the historical funding of climate skeptics — which was by then extensively documented — Exxon's communications team attempted to contest the factual accuracy of the investigative reporting, a strategy that proved unsustainable.

Most damagingly, ExxonMobil filed a lawsuit in Texas state court seeking to block a subpoena from Massachusetts Attorney General Maura Healey — a move widely interpreted as an attempt to use litigation to suppress disclosure rather than engage with the substance of the investigation. The lawsuit generated a new round of hostile coverage and reinforced the narrative that Exxon was obstructing accountability. The company's posture throughout — denial, attack, and litigation — failed to distinguish between what was defensible (its current climate positions) and what was not (its historical conduct).

ExxonMobil also made no early effort at partial acknowledgment — no statement recognizing that its historical funding of third-party denial groups had been inconsistent with good corporate governance, no expression of regret for contributing to public confusion on climate science. The absence of any accountability gesture left no credibility reservoir to draw on. By treating every inquiry as an attack to be repelled rather than a legitimate question to be engaged, the company's communications accelerated rather than contained the reputational crisis.

### What Partially Worked

ExxonMobil successfully fought the New York investigation to a favorable outcome: in 2019, a New York state court ruled that the AG had not proven securities fraud under the Martin Act. This legal result was a genuine win, though it did not resolve the underlying reputational damage or deter other jurisdictions from pursuing similar claims. The company's legal team performed well even as the communications function failed. ExxonMobil also used shareholder meetings to demonstrate that its current research programs acknowledged climate risk — a narrow, defensible position that prevented the crisis from expanding into questions about present-day conduct.

## Outcome & Legacy

- New York's Martin Act case was dismissed in 2019, but the "Exxon Knew" narrative became permanent institutional reputational damage
- The crisis accelerated institutional investor pressure on climate disclosure across the oil and gas sector
- In 2021, activist hedge fund Engine No. 1 won three seats on ExxonMobil's board with a campaign centered on climate strategy failures — a direct legacy of the credibility collapse that began in 2015
- Multiple other climate liability lawsuits against ExxonMobil and other oil majors followed, citing the internal documents surfaced by the 2015 investigations
- The case established "what did they know and when did they know it" as the central framework for corporate climate accountability litigation globally
- ExxonMobil's decades of climate denial funding were formally documented in congressional testimony and academic studies, creating a permanent historical record

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Internal knowledge that contradicts public positions creates catastrophic long-term exposure.** Documents do not stay secret indefinitely. Any organization whose internal research contradicts its public narrative must either align its narrative with the evidence or accept that eventual exposure will be far more damaging than early disclosure.
2. **Funding third parties to carry your denied position is not a firewall.** Exxon's use of think tanks and research institutes to spread climate doubt created a documented paper trail just as damaging as if the company had made the claims directly.
3. **Attacking investigators amplifies the story.** CEO Rex Tillerson's characterization of the AGs as politically motivated gave the investigation sustained news cycle life. Acknowledgment and engagement — even without concession — would have been less damaging.
4. **Litigation as a communications tool can backfire.** Filing suit to block a subpoena reinforced the exact narrative (cover-up, obstruction) the company was trying to deny.
5. **Securities law has a long memory.** The gap between internal documents and public investor disclosures creates fraud exposure that can surface years or decades after the underlying conduct.
6. **Historical conduct cannot be fully defended by current conduct.** Pointing to Exxon's present-day climate research did not neutralize the historical record. Partial acknowledgment of past errors, offered early, typically reduces long-run reputational damage.
7. **Governance consequences arrive slowly but reliably.** The Engine No. 1 board victory in 2021 — six years after the original reporting — demonstrates that reputational crises unresolved in the acute phase continue to generate institutional consequences.
