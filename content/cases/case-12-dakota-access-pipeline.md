## The Crisis

The Dakota Access Pipeline (DAPL) was a $3.8 billion, 1,172-mile crude oil pipeline designed to carry Bakken shale oil from North Dakota to Illinois. Energy Transfer Partners (ETP) routed the pipeline to pass under Lake Oahe on the Missouri River — the primary drinking water source for the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe — and through land the tribe considered unceded treaty territory under the 1851 and 1868 Fort Laramie Treaties. The Army Corps of Engineers initially approved the route without conducting a full Environmental Impact Statement, and the tribe's formal objections were either dismissed or ignored during the permitting process.

The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe and allied Indigenous nations established a protest encampment — the "Sacred Stone Camp" — beginning in April 2016, framing the pipeline as both an environmental threat to their water supply and a violation of their treaty rights and cultural heritage. By summer 2016, the camp had grown into one of the largest gatherings of Native American tribes in over a century, with representatives from more than 300 Indigenous nations present. The protest quickly became a global phenomenon: under the hashtag #NoDAPL, support flooded social media, and in November 2016 approximately 1.4 million people "checked in" to the Standing Rock location on Facebook in a symbolic act of solidarity.

ETP's response throughout was to treat the protest as a trespass and law enforcement problem rather than a communications or stakeholder engagement challenge. In August 2016, private security contractors hired by the company used pepper spray and attack dogs on protesters who had moved onto the pipeline right-of-way — footage of the dogs drawing blood circulated globally and produced an immediate and severe reputational backlash. In November 2016, authorities used water cannons on protesters in sub-freezing temperatures, injuring over 300 people, in an incident that generated international condemnation.

In December 2016, the Obama administration halted construction and ordered the Army Corps to conduct a full environmental review of the Lake Oahe crossing. President Trump reversed this by executive order in January 2017, and the pipeline was completed and began operations in June 2017. However, in 2020 a federal judge ruled that the Army Corps' original environmental review had been inadequate and ordered a new Environmental Impact Statement, requiring the pipeline to cease operations pending that review — a partial legal vindication of the tribe's core argument, years after the most visible phase of the crisis had ended.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

ETP CEO Kelcy Warren gave combative media interviews throughout the crisis, dismissing the tribe's concerns as obstructionist and politically motivated. Warren showed no willingness to engage with the substance of the water rights or treaty rights arguments, framing the conflict entirely in terms of the company's legal permits. This legalistic posture — "we have the permits, we are building the pipeline" — was legally defensible but was catastrophically inadequate as a communications strategy in a crisis driven by moral authority and global public sympathy.

The company's sustained use of "trespass" language to describe protesters dehumanized demonstrators and inflamed public sentiment. The decision to deploy private security with dogs — an image that evoked the civil rights era attacks in Birmingham, Alabama — was either not anticipated or not prevented by whoever held communications oversight at ETP. There is no evidence the company had a social media monitoring function adequate to understand what was happening in real time. The company never acknowledged the FPIC (Free, Prior and Informed Consent) framework or engaged a third-party mediator. No senior ETP executive visited Standing Rock in a non-adversarial capacity during the acute phase of the crisis.

### What Partially Worked

The company did maintain consistent messaging about its legal permits and the project's economic importance — internal stakeholder communications (investors, contractors) remained coherent. The pipeline was ultimately completed, meaning ETP achieved its operational objective. However, achieving that objective required a presidential intervention and came at the cost of lasting reputational damage, ongoing litigation, and the precedent that an inadequate environmental review process could unwind a completed pipeline years later.

## Outcome & Legacy

- Pipeline was completed and operational but subject to ongoing legal challenges
- 2020 federal court ruling ordered a full Environmental Impact Statement, validating the tribe's arguments years later
- ETP incurred significant legal costs and reputational damage with ESG-focused investors
- The crisis became a landmark case study in FPIC failure and the amplifying power of social media in indigenous rights disputes
- The Standing Rock protest directly influenced the Biden administration's review of pipeline permits and influenced ESG policy frameworks globally
- Several major banks that had financed DAPL faced sustained shareholder pressure over their participation
- The case is now routinely cited in international law discussions about indigenous consultation obligations

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Legal compliance is not sufficient for social license.** Having permits does not mean a project has community consent, and treating them as equivalent is a communications failure with operational consequences.
2. **FPIC is both a legal obligation and a strategic necessity.** Failure to conduct Free, Prior and Informed Consent processes creates an exposure that cannot be managed reactively once the crisis ignites.
3. **Security force decisions are communications decisions.** The use of dogs and water cannons were operational choices that became defining images of the crisis. Communications leadership must have authority over security contractor conduct near protest sites.
4. **Social media amplification changes the speed of crises.** 1.4 million Facebook check-ins happened in hours. The company had no real-time social media response capability adequate to the scale of the conversation.
5. **CEO combativeness accelerates reputational damage.** Kelcy Warren's dismissive tone in media interviews provided ongoing fuel for critics. A posture of "we hear the concerns and are investigating" — even without policy concession — would have been less damaging.
6. **Early third-party engagement can prevent escalation.** Bringing in a neutral mediator or respected Indigenous leader as a facilitator in April 2016 might have created a genuine dialogue. No such step was taken.
7. **Winning operationally while losing reputationally creates long-term costs.** ETP built the pipeline but the reputational and legal legacy has imposed continuing costs — a reminder that short-term operational victory is not the same as crisis resolution.

## Latin America Application

| Mistake | LATAM Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| No meaningful FPIC process before project announcement | In LATAM, indigenous consultation is legally required under ILO Convention 169 and national law in Mexico, Peru, Colombia, Chile; failure exposes projects to injunctions and international litigation | Conduct documented FPIC processes before public project announcements; engage communities as stakeholders, not obstacles |
| Private security contractors used near protest sites | High risk in LATAM where security force incidents involving mining and energy companies have triggered national crises (e.g., Tía María, Peru) | Establish clear protocols separating private security from community engagement; ensure communications team has authority over security contractor behavior |
| "Trespass" framing for protesters | Criminalizing protesters in LATAM inflames media coverage and invites NGO mobilization; governments are often politically unable to defend companies that use this framing | Frame community concerns as legitimate even when disagreeing with specific demands; avoid legalistic language in public statements |
| CEO combativeness in media | LATAM media ecosystems amplify executive tone; combative CEO statements become opposition campaign material | Coach senior executives for empathetic messaging during acute phases; consider designating a Community Relations VP as primary spokesperson |
| No social media monitoring during escalation | #NoDAPL-style movements can organize globally within hours; LATAM projects face similar risk with platforms like Twitter/X and WhatsApp | Implement real-time social media monitoring from project announcement; build rapid-response capability before it is needed |
