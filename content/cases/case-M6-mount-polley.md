## The Crisis

On August 4, 2014, the earthen tailings storage dam at Imperial Metals' Mount Polley copper and gold mine in British Columbia's interior collapsed without warning. Approximately 25 million cubic metres of tailings slurry and process water — a volume equivalent to roughly 10,000 Olympic swimming pools — cascaded through Hazeltine Creek, into Polley Lake, and ultimately into Quesnel Lake, one of the cleanest and deepest lake systems in British Columbia and a critical sockeye salmon habitat. It was the largest tailings dam failure in Canadian history.

The 2015 Independent Expert Engineering Investigation and Review Panel, commissioned by the BC government, determined the root cause was a design flaw: an undiscovered layer of glaciolacustrine (glacial lake-bottom) clay beneath the dam's foundation was not identified in original geotechnical assessments. This weak layer gave way under the sustained load of the tailings storage facility. The panel's report was unambiguous — the failure was preventable and attributable to flawed design, not a sudden geological event beyond reasonable anticipation. The findings had global implications for tailings dam safety standards and contributed to the development of the 2020 Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management.

Imperial Metals CEO Brian Kynoch issued a public statement within hours of the breach and was visibly present at media briefings in the days that followed — a faster and more personally accountable response than seen in comparable cases such as Samarco (2015) or Vale's Brumadinho (2019). The BC government ordered the immediate cessation of all mining operations at the site. A drinking water advisory was issued for downstream First Nations communities. However, a critical failure emerged rapidly: Secwépemc and Carrier Sekani First Nations communities whose water sources and traditional territories were directly affected were not formally notified for several hours after the breach — in some accounts, learning of the event through media channels before receiving any communication from the company or the provincial government.

The path to any accountability proved long and ultimately inconclusive. In 2019, BC's Environmental Assessment Office recommended charges against Imperial Metals and the BC government (whose regulators had approved the dam design) under the Mines Act and Environmental Management Act. Charges were eventually stayed in 2021 due to unconstitutional delays in the prosecution process — meaning no financial penalties were ever enforced. The mine was permitted to resume partial operations in 2015, less than a year after the breach, while full remediation of the affected lake and creek systems remained incomplete. This decision to resume operations inflamed affected communities and NGOs and became a defining second chapter of the reputational crisis.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

The most consequential communications failure was the delayed notification of First Nations communities. The Secwépemc Nation and Carrier Sekani Tribal Council — whose members relied on Quesnel Lake and Hazeltine Creek for drinking water, fishing, and cultural practices — were not contacted in a timely and direct manner despite being the most immediately affected parties. Under the principle of Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) and existing regulatory consultation obligations, direct, immediate communication was not optional. The company's initial notification prioritized regulators and media before indigenous rights holders.

The BC government's decision to allow Imperial Metals to resume operations in 2015 — before independent environmental monitoring confirmed remediation — severely eroded the "mixed" positive elements of the initial response. The company's communications framing around resumption focused on economic rationale and regulatory approval rather than genuine community readiness. First Nations and environmental groups viewed the re-opening as confirmation that economic interests would override environmental accountability regardless of declared commitments.

The stayed prosecution — while a legal outcome rather than a communications decision — compounded long-term reputational damage. The absence of any financial penalty reinforced a public narrative that large extractive companies in Canada face no meaningful legal consequences for catastrophic environmental events affecting indigenous communities. The company never developed a sustained, transparent public engagement process in the years following the breach.

### What Partially Worked / What Worked

CEO Brian Kynoch's rapid public appearance and willingness to engage with media in the immediate aftermath represented a meaningful contrast to the silence or delayed CEO visibility that characterizes many mining crises. The acknowledgment that the breach was serious — rather than minimizing or deflecting — was appropriate in tone. The company cooperated with the Independent Expert Panel process and did not publicly contest the panel's findings attributing the failure to design flaws. Imperial Metals also engaged with remediation contractors relatively promptly and funded ongoing environmental monitoring at the site.

## Outcome & Legacy

- No financial penalty was ever successfully enforced against Imperial Metals despite the largest tailings dam failure in Canadian history.
- Quesnel Lake's full ecological recovery remained uncertain years after the event; long-term monitoring programs were established but remediation of Hazeltine Creek was extensive and ongoing.
- The Independent Expert Panel report became a foundational reference document for global tailings dam safety reform, contributing directly to the MAC (Mining Association of Canada) and ICMM's 2020 Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management.
- The case became a benchmark for indigenous notification failures in Canadian mining; it accelerated discussion about mandatory, real-time notification protocols for First Nations communities adjacent to mine infrastructure.
- Imperial Metals' share price dropped approximately 50% in the weeks following the breach and did not fully recover for several years.
- The case is regularly cited in Canadian environmental law and indigenous rights scholarship as an example of regulatory and corporate governance failure operating simultaneously.

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. Speed of CEO visibility matters, but it cannot substitute for targeted stakeholder notification. Impacted indigenous communities must receive direct, proactive contact before media statements are issued.
2. A rapid initial response can be undermined entirely by subsequent decisions. Imperial Metals' relatively strong first 48 hours were erased by the 2015 resumption of operations and the absence of enforced accountability.
3. Free, Prior and Informed Consent is not only a pre-project obligation — it applies to incident response. Failing to notify rights-holding communities immediately in a crisis involving their water and territory is a human rights failure, not merely a communications gap.
4. Regulatory approval to resume operations does not equal social license to resume operations. Companies that conflate the two invite a second crisis wave.
5. The absence of financial penalties, while a legal outcome, becomes a communications liability. Companies in contested legal processes should develop a narrative around genuine remediation commitment that is independent of legal outcome.
6. Independent expert commissions can be a credibility asset if the company does not contest credible findings. Imperial Metals gained some credit by not fighting the panel's design-flaw conclusion.
7. Tailings infrastructure risk is a forward-looking communications obligation. Companies must be able to demonstrate ongoing due diligence to stakeholders before failures occur, not only in response to them.
