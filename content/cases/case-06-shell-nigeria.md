## The Crisis

Shell began operating in Nigeria's Niger Delta in the 1950s through its joint venture with the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation. The Ogoniland region — home to approximately 500,000 Ogoni people — was one of the most productive oil-producing areas. Decades of extraction left the region severely environmentally degraded: oil spills contaminated rivers, farmland, and drinking water. The Ogoni people received minimal economic benefit while bearing the full environmental cost.

Ken Saro-Wiwa — author, television producer, and activist — founded the Movement for the Survival of the Ogoni People (MOSOP) in 1990. MOSOP organised mass nonviolent protests against Shell's operations and the Nigerian military government. In January 1993, 300,000 Ogoni people — approximately 60% of the population — marched in one of the largest protests in African history.

The Nigerian military government under General Sani Abacha responded with increasing brutality. In May 1994, four Ogoni chiefs were killed. Saro-Wiwa and eight other MOSOP leaders were arrested, tried by a special military tribunal on disputed murder charges, and on November 10, 1995 — the same week Shell's annual general meeting was held — were executed by hanging.

Shell publicly stated it would not intervene in Nigerian legal proceedings, a position maintained as a matter of corporate principle. Internally, the picture was more complex: multiple accounts suggest Shell had prior knowledge of the trial's predetermined outcome, and the company's relationship with the Abacha regime was substantial.

## PR & Communications Response

### The "Non-Interference" Position

Shell's core communications position was that it was a commercial enterprise that did not and should not interfere in the judicial processes of sovereign nations. This position had legal and philosophical coherence. It had no moral coherence in a context where a client government was executing activists for opposing an oil company's operations.

The policy of non-interference was communicated in a vacuum of empathy. Shell never publicly acknowledged the environmental damage its operations had caused in Ogoniland. It never expressed sorrow about Saro-Wiwa's execution. Its statements were legalistic and removed — they read as the communications of an institution that had concluded that the deaths of nine people were a government matter, not a corporate one.

### Silence as Complicity

In the international media — particularly in Europe, where Shell's consumer brand was strongest — institutional silence during a human rights crisis is interpreted as complicity. The absence of any Shell statement acknowledging the injustice of the executions was itself read as endorsement. This is a fundamental communications principle that Shell's advisers either failed to understand or were overruled on: in a crisis with clear moral dimensions, silence is a position, and it is always the worst one.

### The Dual Crisis of 1995

Shell faced two simultaneous international crises in 1995: the Brent Spar occupation and the Saro-Wiwa execution. Both reached peak media intensity within months of each other. The combination overwhelmed Shell's crisis communications capacity and created a mutually reinforcing narrative: Shell was a company that would dump toxic waste in the ocean and allow governments to execute its critics. Neither story was being managed; together, they were catastrophic.

### The Long Campaign

What Shell's communications team failed to anticipate was the durability of the Saro-Wiwa case as an organising narrative. International human rights organisations — Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, PEN International — sustained the campaign for years. Academic institutions published research on Shell's complicity. The case was taught in business schools and law schools globally. The Alien Tort Claims Act was used to bring civil suits in US courts.

In 2009 — 14 years after the executions — Shell agreed to a **$15.5 million** out-of-court settlement with the Saro-Wiwa family and other victims, without admitting liability. The settlement provided legal closure but no reputational closure: the story continued to be published, taught, and cited.

### Shell's Post-Crisis Transformation

The combination of Brent Spar and Saro-Wiwa forced Shell into a genuine corporate transformation. Its 1998 publication *"Profits and Principles — Does There Have to Be a Choice?"* was a landmark document: the first major oil company report to explicitly address human rights, sustainable development, and stakeholder responsibility. Shell also began publishing an annual social and environmental report. These were substantive changes, not cosmetic ones — but they came at least three years too late to prevent the reputational damage.

## Outcome & Legacy

- **$15.5 million** out-of-court settlement with Ogoni families in 2009 — no admission of liability
- Ken Saro-Wiwa remains a global symbol of corporate environmental and human rights complicity
- The case is the foundational reference point in all discussions about multinational corporate responsibility in authoritarian host states
- Shell's European consumer brand suffered lasting damage, particularly in the Netherlands and UK
- The case directly shaped Shell's 1998 corporate transformation and contributed to the development of voluntary business and human rights standards globally (which eventually led to the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, 2011)

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Institutional silence during a human rights crisis is interpreted globally as complicity.** Neutrality is not a neutral position when lives are at stake.
2. **Operating in politically unstable environments requires explicit, public human rights commitments.** Companies without a publicly stated policy are left without a communications position when the worst happens.
3. **International NGO networks can sustain campaigns for decades.** The Saro-Wiwa story was being told 20 years after the executions by organisations that had not yet existed in 1995.
4. **Settlement without admission provides legal but not reputational closure.** Every subsequent anniversary of the executions is a news story regardless of the settlement.
5. **Environmental and social license to operate are inseparable.** Shell held a legal concession in Nigeria; it lost its social license from the Ogoni people in the 1970s and never recovered it.
6. **Dual simultaneous crises require a prioritised response strategy.** Shell fought Brent Spar and Saro-Wiwa simultaneously with inadequate resources for either.

## Latin America Application

| Shell Nigeria Pattern | Latin America Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Operating in areas with authoritarian government relationships | Energy firms in LatAm frequently operate in regions with weak rule of law, paramilitaries, or government security forces | Publish explicit human rights policy before operations; train security providers on human rights standards; monitor government-community tensions independently |
| Silence as complicity during community conflict | In LatAm, community-corporate conflicts escalate rapidly to violence; a company's silence is assumed to endorse the more powerful party | Establish rapid response human rights communications protocol; be prepared to publicly distance from state security responses within hours |
| Long NGO campaign durability | LatAm human rights organisations (FIDH, CEJIL, Amazon Watch, WOLA) have 30+ year track records of sustained international campaigns | Map NGO landscape before market entry; engage proactively with credible civil society organisations rather than treating them as adversaries |
| Environmental damage as underlying grievance | Chevron/Texaco's Ecuador contamination showed that unaddressed environmental harm from decades earlier remains a live PR crisis | Commission independent environmental baseline studies before operations begin; publish results and remediation commitments publicly |
