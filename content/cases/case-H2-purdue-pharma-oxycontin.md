## The Crisis

### Origin: OxyContin Launch (1995–2001)

OxyContin (oxycodone hydrochloride) received FDA approval in 1995. It was marketed by Purdue Pharma as a breakthrough pain management drug with a unique selling proposition: a time-release formulation that would provide 12-hour pain relief with lower addiction risk than immediate-release opioids. This claim — that OxyContin was meaningfully less addictive — became central to Purdue's marketing strategy.

By **2001**, OxyContin was the most-prescribed Schedule II controlled substance in the United States.

**The foundational claim was false.** Purdue's own studies showed that for many patients, the time-release mechanism wore off in 6–8 hours, not 12, creating intense cravings and withdrawal symptoms between doses. Prescribers were not told this. Instead, Purdue's salesforce — instructed to downplay addiction risk and aggressively expand prescribing — told physicians the drug was appropriate for moderate pain, not only severe chronic pain.

### The Marketing Machine

Purdue deployed an unprecedented pharmaceutical sales strategy:

- Paid approximately 5,000 physicians, pharmacists, and nurses to speak at "educational" events promoting OxyContin
- Offered financial incentives to sales representatives for increasing prescriptions in their territories
- Targeted high-prescribing doctors in regions with high rates of physical labor (Appalachia, mining communities, rural areas)
- Distributed branded items including plush toys, music CDs, and fishing hats featuring the OxyContin name to patients and healthcare providers
- Provided free "starter" coupons for first-time OxyContin prescriptions

### Scale of Harm

- Approximately **900,000 opioid-related deaths** in the United States since 1999 (CDC data; includes all opioids, not only OxyContin — but Purdue's marketing expansion drove the initial prescription culture that enabled the broader epidemic)
- OxyContin became a gateway: when prescription access was restricted, many users transitioned to heroin and, later, to illicitly manufactured fentanyl
- Rural and working-class communities — particularly in Appalachia, the Midwest, and rural New England — were disproportionately devastated
- The opioid epidemic was the primary driver behind the first sustained decline in US life expectancy in modern times (2014–2017)

## PR & Communications Response

### The Sackler Family Strategy: Philanthropy as Cover

The Sackler family — who owned Purdue Pharma entirely as a private company — pursued a deliberate philanthropic strategy that leveraged cultural institutions to create reputation insulation while liabilities mounted. Sackler-named wings at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Louvre, the Smithsonian, the Tate Modern, Harvard, Oxford, and dozens of other institutions generated positive media coverage and social capital that made the family appear as generous cultural benefactors.

When the opioid crisis became undeniable — driven by investigative journalism, congressional testimony, and massive state attorney general investigations — the philanthropic association became catastrophic. Institutions began removing the Sackler name. The process of name removal generated sustained media coverage of exactly what the philanthropy had been designed to prevent: sustained public scrutiny of the family.

**The lesson:** Reputational capital built on philanthropy while harmful practices continue is not only unsustainable — when it collapses, it collapses publicly, in stages, over years, generating a continuous stream of damaging headlines.

### What Failed

**"We Followed the Science" Defense:** Purdue maintained for years that its marketing accurately reflected OxyContin's pharmacological profile and that any misuse was the fault of patients and illicit distributors, not the company. This defense was contradicted by the company's own internal documents, surfaced in discovery.

**Denial of Addiction Risk:** The company's consistent public position — that OxyContin's addiction rates were lower than comparable opioids — was the foundational fraud. When internal documents showed executives knew about abuse patterns years before public disclosures, the "we didn't know" defense collapsed.

**Asset Extraction While Liabilities Grew:** Between **2008 and 2018**, the Sackler family withdrew approximately **$11 billion** from Purdue Pharma — as state attorneys general were filing suits and opioid deaths were rising. The timing of the withdrawals — accelerating as legal exposure became visible — created exactly the impression of deliberate asset protection ahead of anticipated liability. The bankruptcy filing in 2019 was viewed, widely, as the final step in that strategy.

**International Expansion (Mundipharma):** Through Mundipharma, the Sackler family's international pharmaceutical network, similar OxyContin marketing strategies were deployed in countries including Australia, Brazil, Colombia, and across Europe and Asia — reproducing the same aggressive, minimization-of-risk messaging that had driven the US crisis. This gave the crisis a global dimension and created additional legal exposure in multiple jurisdictions.

**The Bankruptcy as Communications Crisis:** Purdue's 2019 bankruptcy filing was framed by the company as a mechanism to channel resources to victims. Critics — including multiple state attorneys general — argued it was designed to achieve a global settlement that would limit Sackler family personal liability. The distinction between "restructuring to pay victims" and "using bankruptcy to protect family wealth" dominated media coverage of the proceedings for four years.

## What Actually Happened

The 2007 guilty plea — with its $635 million fine — was initially treated by the company as a managed legal resolution. Purdue continued operating. Marketing practices changed modestly. OxyContin prescriptions remained high. The epidemic accelerated.

The 2020 guilty plea and bankruptcy brought operational cessation. Purdue Pharma was dissolved. A new entity — Knoa Pharma — emerged from the restructuring, focused on addiction treatment rather than pain management. The Sackler family reached a $7.4 billion settlement without admitting wrongdoing. Legal debate about the adequacy of that settlement — and whether the bankruptcy process appropriately protected the family from personal liability — continued into 2025.

## Lessons for Minerba Consultants

1. **Corporations cannot sustain a false foundational claim indefinitely.** When the claim is embedded in every marketing document, every sales call, and every scientific presentation — and internal documents exist showing executives knew it was false — the eventual collapse of the defense is total, not partial.

2. **Philanthropy-as-reputation-management is uniquely fragile.** The Sackler name removal process became a sustained, publicly visible rebuke that generated more media coverage than the initial donations. For clients considering "offsetting" harmful operations with cultural philanthropy: it does not neutralize accountability; it concentrates future exposure.

3. **Asset extraction ahead of anticipated legal liability will be discovered and will become the story.** $11 billion withdrawn over ten years while opioid deaths rose is not a financial planning decision — it is a visible narrative about priorities.

4. **International expansion of a flawed product or practice replicates liability globally.** Mundipharma's marketing in Latin America exposed the Sackler network to multiple additional jurisdictions. For companies operating in multiple countries: legal containment strategies that work in one jurisdiction may not transfer, and a global settlement is far harder to manage than a domestic one.

5. **In Latin American contexts:** Several Latin American countries — including Brazil and Colombia — have faced their own opioid advocacy crises driven partly by Mundipharma's local operations. This case is relevant to healthcare industry clients considering similar aggressive marketing of controlled substances in markets with limited regulatory infrastructure.
