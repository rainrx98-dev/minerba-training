## The Crisis

On November 5, 2015, the Fundão tailings dam at the Germano iron ore complex — operated by Samarco Mineração, a 50/50 joint venture between BHP Billiton and Vale — collapsed near the town of Mariana, in Minas Gerais, Brazil. **19 people died.** The dam failure sent an estimated 43.7 million cubic meters of iron ore tailings into the Gualaxo do Norte river, then the Carmo river, and ultimately the Rio Doce — one of Brazil's most important river systems — flowing 650 kilometers to the Atlantic Ocean.

The town of Bento Rodrigues, located downstream, was almost entirely destroyed. Approximately 700 people lost their homes. Downstream communities along the Rio Doce were cut off from their primary water source. The toxic mud killed fish, contaminated drinking water, and reached the Atlantic coast near Regência, Espírito Santo, coating beaches with orange sludge. It was described by Brazilian authorities as the largest environmental disaster in the country's history.

Samarco was jointly owned by two of the world's largest mining companies — BHP Billiton (Australian-British) and Vale (Brazilian, majority state-linked). Both companies had extensive global communications and government relations capabilities.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

**Initial Framing as "Mud Flow":** Early communications from Samarco and its parent companies referred to the release as a "mud flow" and a "mudslide" — language that obscured the toxic nature of the tailings material and the engineered origin of the failure. Journalists and environmental scientists quickly corrected this framing publicly, damaging credibility at the outset.

**Slow CEO Visibility:** Samarco CEO Ricardo Vescovi and the executives of BHP and Vale were not rapidly visible in the affected communities. The communications default was press statements and technical briefings — not human presence in Bento Rodrigues or along the Rio Doce.

**Scale Minimization:** Early company statements significantly underestimated both the geographic extent of contamination and the expected timeline for recovery. Repeated downward revisions of reassuring statements — as the mud plume traveled further downstream week by week — produced a compounding credibility deficit.

**Shareholder-First Signaling:** BHP's early communications visibly prioritized reassurance to institutional investors — statements about insurance coverage, liability limitation, and operational impact at other BHP mines. This order of priority was widely noted and criticized by Brazilian media, civil society, and the government.

**The Renova Foundation Problem:** In 2016, the companies established the Renova Foundation as the vehicle for compensation and environmental remediation. While substantively ambitious, the Foundation became its own PR crisis: slow disbursements, contested victim assessments, and community exclusion from governance turned the remediation body into a symbol of corporate indifference rather than commitment. As of 2022, thousands of victims remained without settlement.

### What Was Done Better

The companies did not attempt to deny responsibility or fight the factual record. A substantial voluntary remediation commitment was made relatively quickly. Samarco eventually suspended operations (partly regulatory, partly voluntary). This prevented the compounding communications disaster of simultaneous fighting legal liability and managing ongoing operations.

---

## What a Better Response Would Have Looked Like

**Within 2 hours:** Joint statement from BHP CEO and Vale CEO — both named — expressing unconditional acknowledgment of the event and personal commitment to affected communities. Samarco's CEO physically in Mariana.

**Within 24 hours:** Suspension of all Samarco operations announced. Environmental monitoring along the Rio Doce corridor activated and made public in real time.

**Within 72 hours:** Community liaison teams deployed to every affected town along the 650 km corridor. Individual family compensation process opened, not routed through a new institution to be designed later.

**Renova Foundation redesign:** The Foundation should have been governed with mandatory community representation, independent auditing, and public disbursement reporting from day one. A compensation vehicle governed exclusively by the companies responsible was predictably contested.

## What Actually Happened

**Legal:** In November 2024, a landmark R$170 billion (~$30 billion USD) reparations settlement was approved, covering federal and state government claims as well as thousands of individual victims. It was the largest corporate settlement in Brazilian history, involving BHP, Vale, and Samarco collectively.

**Renova Foundation:** The Foundation continued operating under persistent criticism for slow victim compensation through 2023. Brazilian courts periodically intervened to force faster disbursements.

**BHP's Position:** BHP, operating from Australia, faced a class action lawsuit in Australian courts (ultimately dismissed on jurisdictional grounds) and in UK courts. The UK case — involving approximately 700,000 claimants — was proceeding as of 2024, representing one of the largest civil claims in legal history.

**Samarco:** The mine resumed limited operations in 2020, more than four years after the collapse, under strict conditions.

## Lessons for Minerba Consultants

1. **In a multi-entity JV crisis, both parents must appear as one voice.** BHP's investor-focused tone and Vale's Brazil-focused response created confusion and the impression that neither company was fully in charge.

2. **The compensation vehicle must be designed for the affected communities, not the responsible companies.** The Renova Foundation's governance structure — controlled by the companies paying into it — made every disbursement delay look like bad faith, regardless of intent.

3. **Environmental disasters that unfold over time (mud traveling 650 km over weeks) require sustained communications operations, not a single press release cycle.** Samarco and its parents lapsed into operational silence as the mud plume continued — allowing others to own the narrative.

4. **"Mud flow" vs. "toxic tailings dam failure": word choice matters acutely.** In the first 72 hours, the language used is the story. Technical euphemism signals cover-up.
