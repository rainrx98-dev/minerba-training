## The Crisis

The La Oroya polymetallic smelter has operated in the Junín region of the central Peruvian Andes since 1922. It processes lead, zinc, copper, silver, and other metals from ores sourced throughout the region. The town of La Oroya grew up around the smelter; many residents' families had worked there for generations.

In 1997, Doe Run Peru — a subsidiary of Renco Group, a US-based private holding company owned by Ira Rennert — acquired the smelter from Peru's state-owned Centromin Peru. With the acquisition came a contractual commitment to an **Environmental Adequacy and Management Program (PAMA)**, which required Doe Run Peru to invest in pollution controls and progressively reduce emissions to legal limits over ten years.

**Doe Run Peru did not meet its PAMA commitments.** Instead, the company sought and received multiple extensions from the Peruvian government, citing financial difficulties, technical complexity, and the economic importance of the smelter to the La Oroya community. The extensions continued for over a decade.

### The Health Crisis

Studies documented the consequences:

- **99% of children tested in La Oroya** had blood lead levels above WHO safe limits
- **Average blood lead level: 33.6 µg/dL** — more than three times the WHO maximum safe limit of 10 µg/dL
- The Blacksmith Institute (now **Pure Earth**) repeatedly listed La Oroya among the **ten most polluted places in the world** (multiple years, 2006–2013)
- Lead contamination causes irreversible neurological damage, developmental delays, and organ failure, particularly in children
- Adult residents reported elevated rates of respiratory illness, cardiovascular disease, and other pollution-related conditions

The contamination was not from a single incident — it was the cumulative result of decades of operation without adequate pollution controls, compounded by Doe Run Peru's failure to implement its contractual remediation commitments.

## PR & Communications Response

### The Core Strategy: Jobs vs. Health

Doe Run Peru's central communications strategy framed the choice facing the community as a false binary: **jobs or health.** The company argued that enforcing environmental standards would require closing the smelter, destroying La Oroya's economy and eliminating the primary source of employment for thousands of families.

This framing was strategically effective in the short term — some La Oroya residents and local union representatives publicly supported the company and opposed environmental enforcement, precisely because of economic dependency on the smelter. The company organized community support events and funded local social programs, creating a visible constituency that appeared to endorse operations.

### What Failed

**The "Jobs vs. Health" Framing Is Ultimately Unsustainable:** When 99% of children have toxic blood lead levels, the "we employ your fathers" argument loses its moral force. The company's communications could not prevent the systematic documentation of harm to children from being the dominant story internationally.

**Repeated Extension Requests:** Each request for a PAMA extension was a visible admission of non-compliance. The pattern — commitment, extension, commitment, extension — destroyed any credibility that the company was acting in good faith toward remediation.

**The Renco Group Distance:** Parent company Renco Group maintained a very low public profile, allowing Doe Run Peru to absorb domestic criticism while Renco's owner Ira Rennert — one of the wealthiest people in the US — avoided media scrutiny. When US media coverage eventually focused on Rennert personally, the contrast between his wealth and La Oroya residents' health outcomes was devastating.

**Bankruptcy Filing:** In 2009–2010, Doe Run Peru filed for bankruptcy protection in Peru, effectively ending operations. The bankruptcy was widely interpreted as the company walking away from its remediation obligations after extracting value from the smelter for 12 years.

**Inter-American Commission on Human Rights:** The Inter-American Commission issued precautionary measures requiring Peru to provide medical treatment to contaminated La Oroya residents. This internationalization of the case demonstrated that domestic Peruvian advocacy had escalated to hemispheric human rights forums — a communications catastrophe that signals total loss of social license.

---

## The Landmark 2024 Ruling

On **March 22, 2024**, a Peruvian court issued a landmark ruling holding the **Peruvian state** responsible for failing to protect La Oroya residents' right to health, requiring the government to provide comprehensive medical care, clean water access, and remediation. The ruling focused on state failure — but it also documented the systematic nature of the corporate contamination that made state intervention necessary.

---

## What a Better Response Would Have Looked Like

**At Acquisition (1997):** A genuine, time-bound, independently verified PAMA implementation plan — not a commitment that could be extended indefinitely. Transparent public reporting on pollution levels annually.

**Upon First Health Study Results:** Rather than contesting health studies, commissioning and publishing independent health monitoring results. Communities that can see their children's health data in real time retain agency; communities that learn their data from NGOs and international media lose trust in the company entirely.

**On the Jobs Framing:** A responsible approach would have been: "We employ this community AND we have an obligation to protect this community's health. These are not in conflict. Here is our investment plan to achieve both." This is harder, more expensive, and more credible than the binary framing.

## Lessons for Minerba Consultants

1. **Inherited liability requires an audit-first strategy.** Doe Run Peru acquired a contaminated smelter with environmental commitments. Companies acquiring existing operations must conduct thorough community health and environmental audits before closing, and build remediation costs into acquisition economics — not treat them as discretionary.

2. **The jobs-versus-environment false binary is a short-term communications tool with long-term costs.** It works until documented harm to children makes the binary morally untenable. At that point, the company has no credible position.

3. **If you cannot fulfill your environmental commitments, say so early, transparently, and with a revised plan that is independently monitored.** Serial extensions appear as deliberate non-compliance. A single, honest, early revision with third-party accountability is far less damaging.

4. **When an international human rights body issues precautionary measures, you have already lost the narrative.** The IACHR involvement signaled to international media, investors, and governments that domestic accountability mechanisms had completely failed. Prevention requires proactive engagement with communities and regulators — not waiting for external compulsion.

5. **In Peru and Latin American mining contexts, community health data belongs to communities.** Controlling or contesting health study access is a losing strategy. Companies that publish their own monitoring data, openly, build the only kind of trust that survives long-term scrutiny.
