## The Crisis

On January 18, 2019, a clandestine tap on a PEMEX Tuxpan-Azcapotzalco gasoline pipeline was detected in the municipality of Tlahuelilpan, in the state of Hidalgo, Mexico. The pipeline had been punctured as part of the widespread practice of *huachicoleo* — the illegal tapping of fuel pipelines, a phenomenon that cost PEMEX an estimated 66.5 billion pesos in losses in 2018 alone and had become endemic across central Mexico. The rupture generated a large gasoline spill and a visible fuel geyser.

What happened next transformed a criminal incident into a humanitarian catastrophe. Hundreds of residents from the surrounding community — including families with children — gathered at the rupture site to collect the spilling gasoline in buckets, barrels, and containers. Images and videos of the scene spread rapidly on social media before the explosion occurred, showing the scale of the crowd and the casual, community-normalized character of the fuel collection. Witnesses later reported that Mexican Army troops were present in the area and were aware of the crowd. Neither PEMEX nor the military ordered or enforced a clearance of the area around the ruptured pipeline.

At approximately 6:45 PM, the gasoline ignited. The fireball that erupted was immediate and total. People who had been standing in pools of fuel were engulfed in flames. At least 137 people died — many at the scene, many more in the days and weeks following from burns sustained in the explosion. The death toll made it the deadliest pipeline incident in Mexico's modern history. Images of survivors engulfed in fire and bodies at the scene were broadcast globally within hours and became among the most harrowing of any industrial disaster in recent Latin American history.

The newly inaugurated government of President Andrés Manuel López Obrador (AMLO), who had taken office on December 1, 2018, was immediately drawn into the crisis. López Obrador had made the fight against *huachicoleo* a centerpiece of his first weeks in office, including a controversial strategy of closing major pipelines to deter theft — a policy that had itself triggered fuel shortages in several states in early January 2019. Tlahuelilpan became an immediate test of that strategy and of AMLO's government.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

**The Huachicoleo Narrative as Deflection:** PEMEX's initial and sustained communications framing centered on the illegality of the fuel theft activity. The company's statements and official communications consistently positioned community members as illegal actors and the explosion as a consequence of their own criminal behavior. While the fuel tapping was unquestionably illegal, this framing came at the direct expense of any acknowledgment of PEMEX's and the military's operational failure to clear the area once the tap and the crowd were known. The deflection was transparent to journalists, civil society organizations, and international observers within hours.

**Military and Operational Responsibility Not Acknowledged:** Mexican Army troops were confirmed to be in the area during the period when hundreds of people were gathering around the ruptured pipeline. The specific chain of command decisions — why the area was not cleared, why the crowd was not dispersed, who had authority to act and chose not to — were never addressed in PEMEX's public communications. This silence was interpreted not as caution but as institutional protection of the military, an especially sensitive framing given the political relationship between the AMLO government and the armed forces.

**Presidential Defense of the Response:** In the days following the explosion, President López Obrador initially defended the overall government response to *huachicoleo*, characterizing the tragedy as evidence of the poverty and desperation that drove communities to collect fuel. While sympathetic in tone, AMLO's framing simultaneously validated the poverty explanation and avoided direct accountability for the failure to clear the site. It also placed the presidential office in the communications chain, which constrained PEMEX's ability to communicate independently or to acknowledge fault without implicating the government.

**Absence of Victim-Centered Communications:** PEMEX's formal communications made no early commitment to victim support, survivor assistance, or family notification protocols. The company's communications infrastructure was not oriented toward affected families. Local hospitals were overwhelmed with burn victims, and families searching for information about the missing had no structured channel to PEMEX for assistance. Civil society organizations and local government officials filled the gap, further marginalizing PEMEX's institutional voice.

**No Community Relations Context:** The communities near PEMEX pipeline infrastructure in Hidalgo had a long history of strained relations with the company over land use, environmental contamination, and lack of economic benefit from the pipelines that ran through their territory. This context — in which fuel tapping had become economically normalized and socially tolerated — was not acknowledged in PEMEX's communications. The failure to address the structural social license deficit that made *huachicoleo* possible meant that PEMEX's narrative offered no pathway to understanding or prevention.

### What Partially Worked

**Speed of Presidential Acknowledgment:** López Obrador addressed the nation within hours of the explosion, expressing condolences and pledging support for victims and families. The speed of presidential communication — whatever its substantive limitations — prevented the political silence that had characterized prior disasters under different administrations.

**Subsequent Investigation:** Mexican authorities announced investigations into the chain of command failures at the site, and some prosecutorial action followed. The investigations did not produce widely reported accountability outcomes but did generate a public record that acknowledged the site management failure as a governmental responsibility.

## Outcome & Legacy

- **At least 137 people killed** — the deadliest pipeline incident in Mexico's modern history
- Many more survivors sustained severe burns requiring long-term medical care
- The disaster occurred less than seven weeks after AMLO took office, becoming the first major crisis test of his administration
- PEMEX's anti-*huachicoleo* enforcement strategy was intensified following Tlahuelilpan, including the closure of major pipeline sections and increased military deployment
- The case produced no major structural changes to PEMEX's community relations or social license frameworks near pipeline infrastructure
- Fuel theft (*huachicoleo*) continued to be a significant operational and financial problem for PEMEX in subsequent years
- The images from Tlahuelilpan became a reference point in international discussions of fuel poverty, infrastructure community relations, and state-owned energy company governance in Latin America
- The case is now cited in Latin American corporate communications training as the paradigm example of deflection-framing failure in a state-owned enterprise context

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Illegal behavior by victims does not eliminate operational accountability.** If your infrastructure, your personnel, and your operational decisions were present at the scene, the question of what the company knew and when it knew it will define the narrative regardless of what third parties did.
2. **The deflection narrative must be airtight to survive scrutiny.** The huachicoleo frame collapsed the moment military presence at the site was confirmed. A deflection that can be undermined by a single confirmed fact is worse than no deflection at all.
3. **State-owned enterprise crisis communications are political communications.** When the government is simultaneously the regulator, the shareholder, and the political actor commenting on the crisis, the company's ability to communicate independently is structurally constrained. This must be planned for in advance, not managed in real time.
4. **Social license deficits are operational risks that must be addressed in communications.** Communities that normalize illegal fuel tapping are communicating something about their relationship with the company operating in their territory. Acknowledging that context — and committing to address it — is both the honest and the strategically correct response.
5. **Victim-centered channels must be established within hours.** When families are searching for information about missing relatives, the absence of a structured company contact point is not neutrality — it is abandonment.
6. **The 72-hour window is compressed in communities with active social media.** In Tlahuelilpan, video of the explosion reached global broadcast before any corporate statement was prepared. The assumption that a company has hours to formulate a response no longer holds.
7. **Community proximity to infrastructure requires ongoing social license investment, not reactive community relations.** Pipeline corridors in Latin America are often in communities with histories of environmental harm, economic exclusion, and distrust of state-owned operators. Waiting for a disaster to engage those communities means engaging from a position of zero or negative trust.

## Latin America Application

| PEMEX Mistake | LATAM Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Huachicoleo deflection — illegal behavior of victims used to avoid operational accountability | Across LatAm, energy companies routinely frame community incidents as security or criminality issues; this framing accelerates NGO and media opposition and delegitimizes the company in regulatory proceedings | Lead communications with operational accountability; acknowledge third-party illegality as a secondary and contextual fact, never the primary frame |
| Military presence at site not addressed | State-owned and state-adjacent energy companies in LatAm routinely operate with military or police presence; when those forces fail to prevent harm, the company's association with state security compounds accountability questions | Develop a separate communications protocol for incidents where security forces were present; address force deployment decisions directly rather than allowing silence to imply coordination or cover-up |
| No community social license framework near pipeline corridor | Fuel theft is endemic in Mexico, Ecuador, Colombia, Venezuela, and parts of Central America — communities near pipeline infrastructure view tapping as a normalized economic activity because the company has provided no positive economic relationship | Implement a pipeline corridor community engagement program: community investment, local employment, environmental monitoring partnerships, and bilateral information protocols |
| Presidential framing constrained PEMEX's accountability communications | In state-owned energy companies across LatAm (ECOPETROL, PETROBRAS, ENAP, YPF), political leadership involvement in crisis framing limits corporate communications flexibility | Establish pre-agreed communications division of authority between company and government shareholder: define which statements are made by government and which by the company, before a crisis, not during one |
| No victim information channel established | In rural LatAm communities, the absence of corporate information channels means families rely on social media, local radio, and political intermediaries — all of which are hostile to the company by default in a disaster context | Pre-build community emergency information protocols: designated bilingual contacts, local radio partnerships, and community leader notification lists for all pipeline corridor communities |
