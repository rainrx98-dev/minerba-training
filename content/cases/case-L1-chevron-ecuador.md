## The Crisis

Texaco operated in Ecuador's Amazon rainforest from 1964 to 1992 as the operator of a consortium that also included the Ecuadorian state company CEPE (later Petroecuador). Over 28 years of operations, the consortium:

- Discharged an estimated **16 billion gallons** of toxic formation water into open pits and rivers
- Spilled approximately **17 million gallons** of crude oil
- Left **916 open waste pits** across 450,000 hectares of rainforest
- Used production practices that were obsolete, below industry standards, and illegal in both Ecuador and the United States — reportedly chosen because they saved approximately $3 per barrel

The Lago Agrio region's indigenous and campesino communities — approximately 30,000 people — used the same rivers and groundwater for drinking, cooking, bathing, and fishing. Cancer rates in the affected area are documented at **150% higher** than the national Ecuadorian average. Specific indigenous groups, including the Tetete people, are believed to have been pushed to extinction in part by the disruption caused by Texaco's operations.

Texaco left Ecuador in 1992 after conducting a $40 million remediation programme. In 1998, the Ecuadorian government signed an agreement accepting this remediation as complete and releasing Texaco from further public-interest claims. Chevron acquired Texaco in 2001 and inherited the liability — and the litigation.

## The Litigation and PR Strategy

### Phase 1: Forum Shopping (1993–2002)

A class-action suit was filed in New York in 1993 on behalf of 30,000 Ecuadorian plaintiffs — *Aguinda v. Texaco*. Texaco vigorously lobbied for the case to be relocated to Ecuador, submitting numerous affidavits praising Ecuador's judicial system as reliable and independent. The case was dismissed for improper venue in 2001. The case was refiled in Ecuador in 2003.

The critical irony: having secured transfer of the case to Ecuador by praising its judicial system, Chevron then spent the next decade attacking the integrity of that same judicial system when it began ruling against them.

### Phase 2: The Ecuadorian Trial (2003–2011)

The trial in Lago Agrio's provincial court lasted eight years. Chevron's communications strategy was built on three pillars:
1. The 1998 remediation agreement absolved all liability
2. Petroecuador — not Texaco — caused most of the ongoing contamination after 1992
3. The plaintiffs' legal team (led by US attorney Steven Donziger) was engaged in fraud

Chevron's legal team was aggressive and expensive: at peak, they deployed approximately **800 lawyers** across multiple jurisdictions globally. This level of legal resource was itself a PR story — the image of a giant corporation deploying hundreds of lawyers against 30,000 indigenous people in a rainforest became a powerful narrative for advocacy organisations.

On February 14, 2011, the Lago Agrio court found Chevron guilty and ordered it to pay **$9.5 billion** in damages (reduced from an initial $18 billion).

### Phase 3: RICO Counter-Attack (2011–2014)

Chevron's response to the $9.5 billion judgment was extraordinary: rather than appealing in Ecuador, it filed a civil RICO (Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act) lawsuit in US federal court against Steven Donziger and his associates, alleging the Ecuadorian judgment was obtained through fraud, bribery, and corruption.

In March 2014, US District Judge Lewis Kaplan ruled that the $9.5 billion judgment was "the product of fraud and racketeering activity" and was unenforceable in US courts. Donziger was ultimately disbarred and spent nearly 1,000 days under house arrest and in detention — becoming an international cause célèbre for legal activists and press freedom organisations.

In August 2018, the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague issued its final ruling: Ecuador had violated international treaties, the 1998 settlement was binding, and Ecuador — not Chevron — was liable. Ecuador was ordered to compensate Chevron for damages.

### Phase 4: UDAPT's "Toxic Tours" — The Counter-Narrative That Persisted

While Chevron pursued its legal strategy, the community organisation UDAPT (Union of Peoples Affected by Texaco) had been running "toxic tours" since 2003. Coordinator Donald Moncayo led journalists, activists, politicians, and celebrities through contaminated sites — open oil pits, rivers, community testimonies, cancer patients. The tours generated:
- Sustained international documentary film coverage
- Global academic and journalistic attention
- Celebrity advocacy (Roger Waters, among others)
- A physical, experiential counter-narrative that no legal brief could answer

The toxic tours ensured that even as Chevron won legal battle after legal battle, the moral story continued to be told and believed. The phrase "Chevron's contamination in Ecuador" remains a fixture of international environmental discourse regardless of the legal outcome.

## The Core PR Paradox

Chevron won the legal war and lost the reputational one permanently. This is the defining paradox of the Lago Agrio case:

**Legal outcome:** Chevron has paid no cleanup funds. The $9.5 billion judgment is unenforceable. Ecuador was ordered to compensate Chevron. Donziger was disbarred.

**Reputational outcome:** Chevron remains globally associated with Amazon contamination and indigenous harm. The case is taught in law schools, environmental science programmes, and business ethics courses worldwide as a case of corporate impunity. Environmental organisations consistently cite it as the definitive example of why investment arbitration enables corporations to escape accountability.

No cleanup has been conducted. The contamination documented in 1993 remains. The communities continue to suffer.

## Outcome & Legacy

- No remediation payments made to affected communities
- $9.5 billion Ecuadorian judgment unenforceable globally
- The Hague arbitration ruled in Chevron's favour on jurisdictional grounds (2018)
- Steven Donziger disbarred; spent 993 days under house arrest and in prison
- UDAPT continues to operate and pursue enforcement in other jurisdictions
- The case is the longest-running and most complex environmental corporate litigation in history
- Chevron's global environmental reputation permanently damaged, particularly in the energy transition era where ESG investors assess precisely this kind of legacy

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Winning legally while losing morally is a permanent reputational condition.** Legal victories do not end reputational crises — they sometimes intensify them by appearing to confirm impunity.
2. **Inherited environmental liability from acquisitions must be assessed as PR risk, not just legal risk.** Chevron's acquisition of Texaco came with a reputational liability that no acquisition due diligence had adequately priced.
3. **Community-organised experiential media ("toxic tours") cannot be defeated by press releases.** Physical reality, accessible to journalists, is the most credible media content available.
4. **Aggressive legal tactics against individual plaintiffs' lawyers generate international solidarity.** The prosecution of Donziger created a secondary global PR crisis around Chevron's conduct that amplified the original story.
5. **Indigenous communities with organised international advocacy sustain campaigns across generations.** UDAPT was founded in 1994 and is still operating. The story does not end.
6. **The 1998 remediation agreement — which "settled" everything — settled nothing.** Legal settlements without actual remediation and community consent are not closures; they are dormant crises.

## Latin America Application

This case IS the Latin America application. Every energy company entering the region should treat Lago Agrio as a scenario planning reference:

| Lago Agrio Pattern | Entering Company Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Operations in indigenous territories with weak environmental enforcement | This describes a large proportion of LatAm energy opportunities — Amazon basin, Andean highlands, offshore near artisanal fishing communities | Commission independent environmental baseline study before operations; publish it; establish public monitoring protocol |
| Remediation agreement that didn't remediate | Government-signed agreements do not create community consent or reputational closure | Engage affected communities directly in any remediation design; community sign-off is the real closure |
| Legal victory generating advocacy momentum | Every court win Chevron achieved generated new international press coverage of the underlying contamination story | Do not treat litigation as a substitute for reputational management; run both strategies in parallel |
| Consulta previa as formality vs. genuine consent | Ecuadorian government signed the 1998 agreement; the people most affected signed nothing | Genuine FPIC requires community participation in design, not just notification. Treat it as an operational requirement, not a compliance checkbox |
| Toxic tours as persistent media | Physical contamination sites are permanent media assets for advocacy organisations | Address the underlying environmental condition — not through PR, but through actual remediation. The only way to end the "toxic tour" story is to end the contamination |
