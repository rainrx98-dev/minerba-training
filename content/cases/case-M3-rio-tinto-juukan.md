## The Crisis

On Sunday, May 24, 2020, Rio Tinto detonated explosives at Juukan Gorge in the Hamersley Range of the Pilbara, Western Australia, destroying two ancient rock shelters — Juukan 1 and Juukan 2. The shelters contained archaeological evidence of continuous human occupation spanning **at least 46,000 years**, including a 4,000-year-old braided hair belt that genetically linked living members of the **Puutu Kunti Kurrama and Pinikura (PKKP) peoples** to their ancient ancestors. The sites were among the oldest known continuously inhabited places in Australia.

Rio Tinto held a **Section 18 consent** under the Western Australian Aboriginal Heritage Act 1972, granted by the WA state government in 2013. This gave legal permission to disturb the sites in order to expand access to the Brockman 4 iron ore deposit. However:

- The full archaeological significance of the shelters — including the 46,000-year timeline — was documented in reports prepared for Rio Tinto by its own archaeologists in 2018–2019, after the consent was granted.
- The PKKP explicitly opposed the blasting in the weeks before it occurred and requested a halt.
- Rio Tinto did not seek an updated consent, did not pause operations, and did not substantively engage with the new archaeological findings in its decision-making.

The explosion was legally authorized. It was morally indefensible — and Rio Tinto's own communications team would spend months explaining why these were separate things, to diminishing effect.

---

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

**Initial Statement: Regret Without Apology:** Rio Tinto's first public statement expressed "regret" — a word that was analyzed and dissected in every subsequent media cycle. It emphasized the company's legal authorization. The framing was: *we did nothing illegal; we regret the cultural loss.* This landed as legalistic minimization of an irreversible cultural destruction.

**CEO Was Invisible:** CEO Jean-Sébastien Jacques did not visit PKKP country, did not meet with PKKP elders publicly and promptly, and was not the visible face of acknowledgment in the first days. The company communicated through statements rather than human presence.

**Board Offered Bonus Cuts, Not Accountability:** When major institutional shareholders demanded executive accountability, Rio Tinto's board initially proposed a combined ~A$7 million reduction in short-term bonuses for the three most responsible executives. **Aberdeen Standard Investments, Australian Super, and Legal & General** publicly rejected this as inadequate. The board was forced to accept resignation agreements within weeks — but the sequencing (bonus cuts first, resignations second, under investor pressure) read as executives protecting their positions until forced out.

**"Never Again" — The Parliamentary Verdict:** The Joint Standing Committee on Northern Australia convened a parliamentary inquiry. Its final report, published in October 2021, was titled ***"A Way Forward"*** — but its proceedings are better known for the phrase *"never again"* that appeared in its interim findings. The inquiry published internal Rio Tinto emails showing employees were aware of the site's significance before the blast. This transformed the story from operational error to willful disregard.

**The Timing Problem:** The blast occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, when physical site visits were restricted. Rio Tinto later cited this as context for why face-to-face community engagement was difficult. The explanation was not accepted. PKKP representatives noted that they had communicated their opposition in writing. Pandemic restrictions did not prevent receiving a letter or responding to one.

### What Was Done Better

Rio Tinto did not attempt to deny the facts. The company cooperated with the parliamentary inquiry, published an internal review, and committed to substantive reforms of its heritage management processes globally. CEO replacements were managed without a prolonged public leadership vacuum. The board eventually accepted full resignations rather than attempting to manage with reduced pay.

## Resignations

Three executives resigned, announced **September 11, 2020**:

| Executive | Role |
|---|---|
| **Jean-Sébastien Jacques** | Chief Executive Officer |
| **Chris Salisbury** | Chief Executive, Iron Ore |
| **Simone Niven** | Group Executive, Corporate Relations |

**Board Chair Simon Thompson** subsequently announced his own departure in March 2022, explicitly citing Juukan Gorge as part of the context.

---

## What Actually Happened

**Regulatory Reform:** Western Australia passed the **Aboriginal Cultural Heritage Act 2021**, replacing the 1972 Act. The new law requires far more robust consultation, gives communities greater power to protect sites, and removes the ministerial consent mechanism that allowed the Juukan authorization. Implementation has been contested, with amendments made before full commencement in 2023.

**Financial Impact:** Rio Tinto's share price was not materially damaged by Juukan in isolation — iron ore commodity prices were strong through 2020, and earnings remained high. This is a widely cited cautionary note: financial markets are poor monitors of social accountability. The reputational cost was substantial but not immediately visible in the stock price.

**Rio Tinto's Global Heritage Processes:** The company committed to overhaul its heritage consultation processes worldwide. In Latin America, this has implications for Rio Tinto's operations and project development in countries with active *consulta previa* obligations (Peru, Chile, Argentina).

---

## Latin American Relevance — Consulta Previa

This case is **directly applicable to Latin American operations** for any mining company. The core failure at Juukan — holding legal authorization while lacking substantive, ongoing consent from the affected indigenous community — mirrors the failure mode in *consulta previa* disputes across the Andes.

**ILO Convention 169**, ratified by most Latin American countries, requires *free, prior, and informed consultation* — not just notification — before projects affecting indigenous territories begin. Key court decisions in Peru, Colombia, Chile, and Bolivia have progressively interpreted this to require:

- Consultation at multiple project phases, not only at initial authorization
- Good-faith negotiation, not box-checking
- Acknowledgment of new information (like the 2018–19 Juukan archaeological findings) as requiring re-consultation

The Juukan case is now cited in Latin American legal proceedings as evidence that a government permit does not substitute for ongoing community consent.

## Lessons for Minerba Consultants

1. **Legal authorization and moral authorization are not the same thing.** When they diverge — especially in indigenous rights contexts — communications that emphasize legality will be destroyed by the moral argument. Acknowledge the moral dimension first.

2. **New material information requires new consultation.** If archaeological or community significance data changes after authorization is granted, proceeding without re-engagement is a governance failure, not an operational choice.

3. **When institutional shareholders demand accountability, the board must lead — not wait.** Rio Tinto's board could have managed the executive transitions proactively and framed them as leadership rather than capitulation. Instead, shareholders led, and the board followed. The difference is enormous for perception.

4. **In Latin America, this is the most teachable case for consulta previa failure.** It is not a LatAm case, but it shows exactly what happens when consultation becomes a bureaucratic step rather than an ongoing relationship.
