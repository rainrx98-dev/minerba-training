## The Crisis

Theranos was a Silicon Valley health technology company founded by Elizabeth Holmes in 2003. Holmes, who dropped out of Stanford at 19, claimed to have developed a revolutionary blood testing platform that could perform hundreds of diagnostic tests from a single finger-prick of blood using a proprietary device called the Edison. The company raised approximately $945 million in funding and was valued at $9 billion at its peak in 2013–2014. Holmes appeared on the covers of Fortune, Forbes, and Inc., and was described as "the next Steve Jobs" — a comparison she deliberately cultivated through her black turtleneck wardrobe and presentation style.

The technology never worked as claimed. Theranos's Edison device could reliably perform only a small number of tests, and those results were frequently unreliable. For the vast majority of tests processed through its Walgreens and Safeway partnerships, Theranos secretly used conventional Siemens and other commercial analyzers, then diluted the small blood samples and ran them through machines designed for larger venous draws — a modification that compromised accuracy. Patients received inaccurate diagnoses for conditions including cancer, HIV, and cardiovascular disease based on flawed results. Some patients made significant medical decisions, including unnecessary procedures and medication changes, on the basis of false Theranos data.

In October 2015, Wall Street Journal reporter John Carreyrou published the first major investigation revealing that Theranos's technology was not what it claimed to be. The story was based in part on information from whistleblowers, including former employees and scientists. The investigation continued over years, culminating in Carreyrou's 2018 book "Bad Blood." The SEC charged Holmes and COO Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani in March 2018 ($500,000 settlement for Holmes; she neither admitted nor denied wrongdoing). The Department of Justice issued criminal indictments against both in June 2018. Holmes was convicted in January 2022 on four counts of fraud and conspiracy and sentenced to 11 years and 3 months in federal prison.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

Holmes's communications response to the Carreyrou investigation was aggressive, litigious, and ultimately catastrophic. In the immediate aftermath of the October 2015 WSJ story, Holmes gave an interview to CNBC's "Mad Money" in which she called the reporting "factually incorrect" and a "huge disappointment." She did not provide specific factual rebuttals but instead appealed to the credibility of her board and the company's mission. She appeared at the Wall Street Journal's own tech conference the day after the story ran and delivered a defiant performance that temporarily halted the stock of publicly traded partners.

Theranos's legal team sent threatening letters to the WSJ before publication and pursued aggressive legal action against whistleblowers, including former employees Tyler Shultz and Erika Cheung. Tyler Shultz — the grandson of Theranos board member George Shultz — described being followed by private investigators and subjected to legal intimidation after he reported concerns to regulators. This pattern of attacking whistleblowers is documented extensively in litigation records and created a secondary narrative of corporate intimidation that intensified media and regulatory attention.

Holmes used her personal narrative as a primary PR asset: the young female founder disrupting a male-dominated field, the dropout visionary, the philanthropic mission to democratize diagnostics. This narrative substituted for technical disclosure for years and was amplified by a board of directors that included former Secretary of State George Shultz, former Secretary of State Henry Kissinger, and former Secretary of Defense James Mattis — figures whose prestige conferred institutional legitimacy but who had no scientific or medical qualifications to evaluate the company's claims. The board's composition was itself a communications strategy.

### What Partially Worked

In the very short term — the 24–48 hours after the October 2015 WSJ story — Holmes's aggressive counter-narrative was effective enough to prevent immediate regulatory action and maintain some investor confidence. Her CNBC appearance and Journal conference performance demonstrated tactical media competence. However, this short-term gain directly amplified the long-term damage: every additional month of operations after the WSJ story extended patient harm and deepened criminal exposure.

## Outcome & Legacy

- Theranos dissolved in September 2018; remaining assets sold.
- Holmes sentenced to 11 years and 3 months in federal prison (reported to serve sentence from May 2023).
- Ramesh Balwani convicted on 12 counts of fraud; sentenced to nearly 13 years.
- The case prompted significant discussion of Silicon Valley "fake it till you make it" culture and the absence of scientific peer review in health technology investment.
- The SEC issued guidance on startup disclosure obligations partly informed by the case.
- George Shultz, who chose Theranos board loyalty over his own grandson's whistleblowing, became a cautionary study in how prestigious board members can lend credibility to fraud.
- Carreyrou's book and the Hulu series "The Dropout" (2022) made the case widely known to non-specialist audiences.
- The case has become a core reference for discussions of celebrity credibility substituting for technical disclosure.

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Personal narrative is not a substitute for technical transparency.** A compelling founder story can delay scrutiny but not survive documented fraud. Every media appearance that substitutes personality for evidence extends criminal liability.
2. **Attacking credible investigative journalism creates a secondary crisis.** Calling the WSJ story "factually incorrect" without providing specific factual rebuttals is a high-risk strategy that fails the moment regulatory action validates the original reporting.
3. **Litigating against whistleblowers inverts the sympathy dynamic.** Using legal resources to intimidate former employees — especially those who reported to regulators — generates a parallel narrative of corporate bullying that attracts congressional attention and media sympathy for accusers.
4. **Board prestige is not a communications asset when credentials are irrelevant.** A board of former secretaries of state and defense provided political legitimacy but zero scientific credibility. When the technical claims failed, the board's composition became evidence of governance failure rather than institutional strength.
5. **Short-term narrative wins extend long-term criminal exposure.** Every week Holmes's denial strategy succeeded in maintaining operations extended the patient harm timeline and deepened fraud charges. The tactically effective CNBC interview is now prosecution exhibit material.
6. **The 72-hour protocol requires honest internal triage before external messaging.** Theranos's communications team could only construct denial messaging because there was no honest internal assessment of what was true. A genuine crisis audit would have forced a different path.
7. **Healthcare fraud is categorically different from product failure.** Patients who received inaccurate diagnoses were directly harmed. Communications strategies built around "our science is still developing" are not available to health companies whose products are already being used on real patients.

## Latin America Application

| Mistake/Lesson | LATAM Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Celebrity narrative substituting for technical disclosure | LATAM health technology markets are rapidly expanding; founder-celebrity dynamics are increasingly common in regional health startups and telemedicine platforms | Counsel health technology clients that regulatory and technical disclosure requirements are non-negotiable regardless of investor narrative; any gap between claimed and actual product performance creates terminal risk |
| Attacking investigative journalists | Investigative journalism on health sector fraud is growing in LATAM (Connectas, La Nación, Infobae); aggressive legal responses to accurate reporting typically amplify rather than suppress stories | Develop protocols for engaging investigative journalists with factual responses; legal threats against accurate reporting are rarely effective and frequently counterproductive |
| Celebrity/political board members as credibility proxies | Similar dynamics exist in LATAM where company boards include former ministers, military officials, or prominent public figures with no technical qualifications | Advise clients that board composition will be scrutinized in a crisis; prestigious non-expert board members become liabilities when technical failures emerge |
| Continuing operations while suppressing safety concerns | Patients in LATAM health systems often have fewer alternative diagnostic options; harm from inaccurate testing can be irreversible | Establish clear internal protocols for halting health product use when accuracy or safety concerns are raised internally, regardless of commercial pressure |
