## The Crisis

### First Spill — September 2015

In September 2015, a valve failure at Barrick's Veladero gold mine in San Juan Province, Argentina, released approximately one million liters of cyanide-laced process solution into the Potrerillos, Taguas, and Jáchal river systems. It was described by Argentine environmental authorities as **the largest mining spill in Argentina's history**.

**The community learned about the spill via employee WhatsApp messages, not from Barrick or regulatory authorities.** Local residents began sharing images and reports before any official communication had been issued. Provincial government officials also received the information through informal channels.

Barrick waited approximately **six days** before making a public disclosure. When the company did respond, its initial characterization called concerns about the spill "unfounded controversy" — a phrase that became emblematic of the company's posture toward affected communities.

Before the spill, Barrick had fired an employee who had raised safety concerns about the mine's operations — a detail that surfaced and was widely reported in the aftermath, framing the company as suppressing internal warnings.

**Regulatory response:** San Juan provincial authorities imposed fines, and the Argentine federal government opened investigations. Barrick acknowledged the spill occurred but contested the severity and the reach of contamination.

### Second Spill — September 2016

Approximately one year later, a second cyanide-related incident occurred at Veladero. The pattern — disclosure delay, community learning through informal channels, official minimization — repeated. Provincial and federal authorities were now operating under sustained public scrutiny. The second incident amplified every criticism made after the first.

### Third Incident — March 2017

A third incident in March 2017 involved abnormally high mercury readings — reportedly 300% above normal levels — in water near the mine. The pattern continued: community alarm, company minimization, regulatory investigation.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

**Suppression Before Disclosure:** The WhatsApp disclosure failure was not simply poor communications — it was a failure of the entire information architecture. When affected communities have no reliable, direct channel to receive mine safety information, they build informal channels. Those channels produce unverified, often alarming information. The company had no ability to correct or contextualize in real time because it had never established the trust infrastructure needed to be believed.

**Six-Day Delay on the First Spill:** In crisis communications terms, six days is an eternity. By the time Barrick spoke officially, the narrative was fully formed — by journalists, by community organizations, by provincial politicians, and by activists. The company's first statement entered a hostile information environment entirely of its own making.

**"Unfounded Controversy" Framing:** Characterizing community concern as "unfounded controversy" — when cyanide had measurably entered river systems used by local communities for water and agriculture — was factually indefensible and strategically catastrophic. It positioned the company against the community rather than alongside it.

**Fired Whistleblower:** The firing of an employee who had raised safety concerns, revealed in the aftermath of the first spill, reframed the narrative entirely: this was not an accident but a pattern of suppressed warning. Crisis communications cannot overcome a factual record that shows advance knowledge and suppression.

**Repeat Incidents Compound Everything:** The September 2016 second spill made it impossible for Barrick to sustain any "we have learned from this" narrative. Three incidents across two years at the same mine constituted a systemic failure, not an operational accident.

**Barrick's Global Context:** Barrick was simultaneously managing a major strategic restructuring under CEO John Thornton, divesting assets and cutting costs. The Veladero incidents occurred as the company was telling investors it had reformed its operations and governance. The Argentine incidents contradicted that narrative directly.

### What Should Have Happened

**Immediate (within hours):** Community notification channels activated, not just regulatory reporting. A dedicated, Spanish-language hotline for the Jáchal valley communities. A company representative physically present in the nearest communities within hours of internal confirmation.

**First 24 hours:** Full disclosure of what was known — including uncertainty — in the local language. No waiting for complete information before communicating. "We know there was a valve failure, we know material entered the river system, we are measuring the extent, we will update you every six hours" is far better than six days of silence.

**Structural:** Before operating, a company in a remote Andean valley needs to have community communication protocols that do not depend on regulatory reporting cycles. WhatsApp will fill the vacuum if the company doesn't.

## What Actually Happened

**Fines:** Argentine provincial and federal authorities imposed fines totaling approximately **$9.8 million USD** — widely criticized as inadequate relative to the scale of environmental impact and Barrick's revenue from Veladero.

**Regulatory:** San Juan provincial authorities briefly suspended Veladero operations after the second spill. Operations eventually resumed under enhanced monitoring conditions.

**Community Relationship:** The Jáchal community and civil society organizations maintained sustained public opposition to Veladero operations. Legal challenges continued in Argentine courts through 2020.

**Barrick's Strategy:** Barrick eventually sold a 50% stake in Veladero to Shandong Gold (a Chinese state-owned mining company) in 2017, complicating both operational and communications accountability further.

## Lessons for Minerba Consultants

1. **In remote mining communities, the company's silence creates an information vacuum that others will fill.** In the age of WhatsApp and social media, there is no such thing as "no comment while we investigate" — there is only "the company is silent while workers are messaging photos." Design proactive community notification as an operational requirement, not a PR afterthought.

2. **"Unfounded controversy" is never the right framing when there is measurable contamination.** The word "unfounded" invites a factual rebuttal. When the community has photos of orange water and dead fish, the rebuttal will be devastating.

3. **Repeat incidents require a different communications architecture than single events.** By the second spill, the company was not managing a crisis — it was managing a relationship in permanent crisis. That requires a dedicated community liaison team, a community advisory board, and sustained transparency — not periodic press releases.

4. **Whistleblower retaliation, discovered post-crisis, reframes everything.** Prior to that revelation, Barrick could plausibly argue the spill was unforeseen. After it, every defense became advocacy against the community. Consult internal warnings seriously — they are also future legal and reputational liabilities.
