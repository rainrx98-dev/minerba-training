## The Crisis

On November 8, 2018, a transmission line owned and operated by PG&E failed near Pulga, California, in Butte County. The resulting fire spread with catastrophic speed driven by dry, wind-driven conditions. The Camp Fire destroyed the entire town of Paradise, California — a community of 27,000 people — along with the neighboring towns of Concow and Magalia. Eighty-five people were killed. More than 19,000 structures were destroyed. It was the deadliest and most destructive wildfire in California's recorded history. The cause was definitively traced to PG&E's Caribou-Palermo transmission line, which had been in service since 1921 and had not received required maintenance inspections.

The Camp Fire did not occur in isolation. In October 2017, PG&E equipment had ignited or contributed to multiple fires across Northern California's wine country, killing 22 people and destroying thousands of structures. The 2015 Butte Fire, which killed two people and destroyed 475 homes, had also been caused by a PG&E power line contacting a tree — an event that produced a $75M settlement but did not trigger the systemic infrastructure overhaul the company's own engineers had recommended. Each fire was a documented warning. PG&E's response to each was to settle, not to fundamentally change its infrastructure investment and vegetation management programs.

The company's financial exposure from the 2017 and 2018 fires was ultimately estimated at more than $30B. Facing that liability with insufficient insurance coverage and a regulatory structure that prevented full cost recovery, PG&E filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on January 29, 2019 — becoming the first US utility to declare bankruptcy as a direct result of climate-related wildfire liability. The filing itself became a major news event, framing PG&E as a company that had chosen financial restructuring over compensating the people whose homes and lives it had destroyed.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

**Operational Updates Instead of Human Acknowledgment:** During and immediately after the Camp Fire, PG&E's public communications focused primarily on operational status updates: which circuits were de-energized, what crews were deployed, how many customers were affected. Families were searching for missing relatives in the rubble of Paradise. The mismatch between PG&E's operational communications register and the human reality of 85 deaths was noticed immediately and criticized widely by journalists, elected officials, and survivors' advocates.

**CEO Departure Under Pressure:** CEO Geisha Williams resigned on January 21, 2019 — eight days before the bankruptcy filing. The timing framed her departure as an escape ahead of accountability. PG&E named interim leadership days before the bankruptcy announcement, creating a visible leadership vacuum at precisely the moment the company needed credible spokespersons. The sequence — CEO out, bankruptcy in — read as institutional abandonment.

**PSPS Communications Chaos:** In October 2019, PG&E initiated its first large-scale Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS) program — deliberately cutting power to more than 2 million customers across Northern and Central California to reduce wildfire ignition risk during high-wind events. The policy itself was defensible. The communications surrounding it were not. Shutoffs were announced with inadequate notice. Customers with medical equipment were not reliably pre-notified. Rural customers with well pumps — their only water source — received the same generic messaging as urban households. Vulnerable populations were insufficiently identified or prioritized. The PSPS program, intended to demonstrate safety leadership, became a second communications crisis layered on top of the first.

**Proactive Disclosure Failures:** PG&E's corporate communications in the years prior to the Camp Fire had not disclosed to investors, regulators, or the public the full scope of its deferred infrastructure maintenance backlog or the assessed probability of catastrophic failure events from aging transmission equipment. When that information emerged through litigation discovery and regulatory proceedings, the gap between internal assessments and public statements became a central element of both the criminal case and the civil litigation.

**Bankruptcy Framing:** The January 2019 bankruptcy filing was announced in a manner that emphasized PG&E's legal and financial position — the need for a "court-supervised process to work through substantial liabilities." Communications to wildfire survivors at the time of filing were widely described as inadequate. Victims' advocates noted that PG&E's first communication priority appeared to be its creditors and investors, not the 85 families whose relatives had died.

### What Partially Worked

**Eventual Settlement Scale:** The $13.5B wildfire victim fund, agreed in 2020 as part of the bankruptcy reorganization, represented one of the largest compensation pools in utility history. The company's willingness to commit to that figure — under court supervision — provided eventual financial relief to survivors, even if the communications process that accompanied the settlement remained impersonal.

**Regulatory Cooperation:** PG&E cooperated with the California Public Utilities Commission's investigations and accepted the resulting oversight conditions, including the appointment of an independent safety monitor. This did not repair public trust but did prevent a prolonged public confrontation with regulators that would have generated ongoing negative coverage.

## Outcome & Legacy

- **85 people killed** in the Camp Fire; thousands more displaced permanently
- **19,000+ structures destroyed**; Paradise, California, eliminated as a functioning community
- **PG&E filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy** in January 2019 — the first US utility bankruptcy driven by climate wildfire liability
- **$13.5B wildfire victim settlement** paid through the bankruptcy reorganization process
- **84 counts of involuntary manslaughter** — PG&E entered guilty pleas on all counts in June 2020
- **PSPS program** exposed inadequate emergency communications infrastructure for vulnerable populations
- California enacted AB 1054, creating the Wildfire Fund and new safety certification requirements for investor-owned utilities
- The case established "climate-driven liability from aging infrastructure" as a quantifiable existential financial risk for utilities globally — a precedent now cited in investor risk disclosures across Europe, Australia, and Latin America

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Operational language is not a crisis communications register.** When people are missing and dying, circuit status updates communicate institutional indifference.
2. **CEO departure during an active crisis compounds every underlying failure.** Timing matters: an exit days before bankruptcy read as strategic abandonment.
3. **Known infrastructure risks that were not disclosed become criminal evidence.** The gap between internal engineering assessments and public statements is not a grey area — it is the basis for prosecution.
4. **Vulnerable populations require differentiated communications protocols.** PSPS failures affecting medical equipment users and rural households demonstrated that mass notification is not crisis communication.
5. **Settlement scale does not substitute for communications quality.** $13.5B in victim compensation did not produce goodwill because the communications process that surrounded it was impersonal and legally cautious throughout.
6. **Deferred maintenance is a communications story before it becomes a disaster story.** Infrastructure companies that do not proactively communicate investment trade-offs cede the narrative entirely when failures occur.
7. **Bankruptcy as a crisis vehicle generates a second-order crisis.** Using financial restructuring to manage liability before fully engaging with victims is perceived — and reported — as corporate interest placed above human consequence.
