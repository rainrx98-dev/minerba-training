## The Crisis

Ørsted A/S, the Danish state-backed offshore wind developer formerly known as DONG Energy, had by 2021 become the world's largest offshore wind company and a flagship of the global energy transition. Its US expansion — anchored by the Ocean Wind 1 and 2 projects off the coast of New Jersey and the Skipjack Wind project off Maryland — was seen as proof that the offshore wind industry could scale into American waters. Ocean Wind 1 alone was a 1,100-megawatt project that New Jersey Governor Phil Murphy had positioned as central to the state's clean energy future.

By 2023, the economics that had underpinned those projects had fundamentally shifted. The Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) for Ocean Wind and Skipjack had been signed in 2019 and 2020, when offshore wind construction costs, turbine prices, and financing rates all sat at very different levels. Between 2021 and 2023, supply chain disruptions — particularly the extended delivery timelines for large offshore wind turbines from manufacturers like Siemens Gamesa and Vestas — drove construction costs far above the locked-in PPA revenues. Rising interest rates from 2022 onward increased project finance costs substantially. The US Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, while generally supportive of clean energy, proved harder for Ørsted to fully monetize in its project finance structures than the company had modeled.

In October 2023, Ørsted announced it was cancelling Ocean Wind 1 and 2 in New Jersey and recording an impairment charge of DKK 28.4 billion (approximately $4 billion). The Skipjack Wind project in Maryland was also cancelled. Ørsted's share price fell approximately 26% in a single day — October 26, 2023 — as the market absorbed the scale of the writedown. By the end of 2023, Ørsted's share price had fallen roughly 60% from its peak in 2021. The cancellations left New Jersey with a significant policy gap and drew furious public statements from Governor Murphy, who characterized the decision as a betrayal of the state's clean energy commitments and demanded the return of a $300 million federal grant that had been associated with Ocean Wind's development.

The Ørsted crisis was not a fraud, an environmental disaster, or an ethical failure — it was a financial failure driven by macroeconomic forces largely outside the company's control. This distinction shaped what the company could and could not do in its communications response.

---

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

CEO Mads Nipper's communications, while more transparent than most comparable crisis responses, underestimated the political dimension of the New Jersey cancellation. Governor Murphy's anger was predictable — Ørsted had been presented as a cornerstone partner in a politically significant state energy program, and the cancellation left him exposed. The company's handling of the New Jersey political relationship in the weeks before and after the announcement was insufficient: Murphy publicly stated he had not received adequate advance notice before the cancellation was announced to investors. The sequence — investor disclosure before political partner briefing — was a standard investor relations priority that was poorly calibrated to the reputational stakes in the New Jersey context.

The scale and speed of the stock price decline — 26% in one day — suggests the market was not adequately prepared for the magnitude of the impairment. Ørsted's investor communications in the months before the announcement had not effectively signaled the deteriorating project economics, meaning the disclosure was experienced as a shock rather than a managed disappointment. In a DKK 28.4 billion writedown, better advance signaling — even without specific project names — would have reduced the acute market reaction.

Ørsted's broader narrative about the future of US offshore wind was also slow to emerge. The company had no clear, pre-prepared "here is what we are doing instead" message ready at the moment of cancellation. This left a vacuum that critics, competitors, and politicians filled with narratives about the viability of US offshore wind as a whole — a sector-level reputational problem that served Ørsted poorly as it sought to preserve its remaining US pipeline.

### What Partially Worked

Nipper and his team were notably transparent about the financial reasons for the cancellations — supply chain inflation, rising interest rates, IRA monetization challenges — in a way that was detailed, credible, and did not attempt to obscure the magnitude of the loss. This transparency was important: it allowed institutional investors, analysts, and energy policy stakeholders to understand the cancellations as the result of macroeconomic forces rather than management failures or concealed problems. The distinction mattered for Ørsted's long-term credibility as a developer.

Ørsted also moved quickly to reaffirm commitment to its remaining global project pipeline, emphasizing that the US cancellations were specific to unworkable fixed-price PPAs rather than a withdrawal from the US market entirely. This framing — "we are exiting these specific projects, not the US" — was clear and correct, and preserved the company's ability to bid on future US projects. The company's communications did not attempt to blame the US political environment in a way that would have damaged future government relationships.

## Outcome & Legacy

- Ørsted recorded total impairments of approximately $4 billion from the US cancellations, the largest single financial event in the company's history
- The stock fell 60% from peak to end-2023, erasing years of investor value
- Governor Murphy's public anger created lasting political complexity in New Jersey for Ørsted's future project participation
- The cancellations triggered a broader reassessment of US offshore wind economics across the sector; competitors including BP, Equinor, and Avangrid also revised or cancelled US projects
- Ørsted preserved its global project pipeline and maintained its position as the world's largest offshore wind developer by installed capacity
- The case established "responsible retreat" as a communications challenge category: how to exit a major commitment without destroying the credibility needed for future commitments
- Fixed-price PPA structures without adequate inflation protection were identified by the industry as a systemic risk; subsequent US offshore wind procurements included more flexible pricing mechanisms

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Investor disclosure sequencing must account for political stakeholder relationships.** When a project is central to a governor's or minister's policy agenda, they should be briefed before — not after — an investor announcement. The cost of managing that political relationship is far lower than the cost of a furious public official demanding grant returns.
2. **Large impairments require advance investor signaling.** A DKK 28.4B writedown announced without preparatory guidance produces a 26% single-day stock decline. Progressive disclosure — signaling deteriorating economics without committing to specific outcomes — reduces acute market shock.
3. **"Responsible retreat" requires a simultaneous "what we are doing instead" message.** Cancelling a major commitment without a ready affirmative narrative creates a vacuum. The crisis communications plan for any potential project exit should include a pre-drafted forward-looking narrative.
4. **Macroeconomic causation is credible but does not eliminate political accountability.** Nipper's transparency about inflation and interest rates was correct, but it did not satisfy political stakeholders whose programs had depended on the project. Both audiences needed to be addressed in parallel, with different messages.
5. **Sector-level narrative spillover is a risk.** Ørsted's cancellations contributed to a broader narrative about US offshore wind viability that affected the whole sector. Companies whose crises have sector-wide implications should actively manage industry-level communications, not just their own corporate narrative.
6. **Preserving future credibility requires explicit commitment language.** Ørsted's clear statement that it was not exiting the US market — only these specific unworkable contracts — preserved its ability to bid on future projects. Ambiguous or overly cautious forward-looking language would have been more damaging.
7. **Contract structure is a communications risk management tool.** Fixed-price PPAs without inflation adjustment created the financial pressure that forced the cancellations. Communicating contract risk structures to investors and policymakers at the time of signing reduces the surprise premium when those risks materialize.

| No advance investor signaling of deteriorating economics | LATAM energy projects often carry country risk and regulatory uncertainty that should be progressively disclosed; sudden impairments damage institutional investor confidence in regional projects | Implement regular transparent investor updates that address project risk factors as they evolve, not only when they crystallize into financial losses |
| Sector-level narrative damage unmanaged | LATAM renewables sectors — particularly offshore wind in Brazil and Chile — are in early development phases; a poorly managed withdrawal by a major developer can slow market development by discouraging future investment | Coordinate with industry associations and other developers when a withdrawal may have sector-level implications; proactive industry-level messaging reduces market-wide damage |
| "Responsible retreat" narrative unprepared | LATAM project exits — particularly from mining and energy projects — frequently trigger community, regulatory, and media crises; exiting without a prepared narrative damages both the specific company and future project viability | Build exit communications plans as a standard component of project risk management; include community transition commitments, regulatory notification protocols, and forward-looking statements |
