## The Crisis

In July 2013, Chinese authorities — specifically the Public Security Bureau (PSB) — announced that they were investigating GlaxoSmithKline's China operations for systematic bribery of doctors, hospital administrators, and government officials. The investigation alleged that GSK China had channeled bribes through a network of travel agencies, medical conferences, speaking fees, and direct cash payments to incentivize prescribing of GSK drugs. The scheme involved hundreds of millions of renminbi funneled through third-party intermediaries to reduce traceability.

Four senior GSK China executives were arrested, including the China general manager at the time. Chinese authorities alleged that the conduct was not only known to local management but had been institutionalized into the sales and marketing operation. The scale was significant: PSB investigators estimated that GSK had distributed approximately RMB 3 billion ($490 million) in bribes over multiple years. In September 2014, a Chinese court convicted five GSK China executives; the company was fined RMB 3 billion — at the time, the largest criminal penalty ever imposed on a foreign company in China.

The scandal immediately triggered investigations in multiple other jurisdictions. GSK faced scrutiny from the U.S. Department of Justice and SEC under the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, as well as investigations in Poland, Iraq, Lebanon, Jordan, and several other countries where similar bribery models were alleged to have operated. The Chinese government's public handling of GSK was widely interpreted as a signal to all multinational pharmaceutical companies operating in China: GSK was made an example, and Chinese authorities subsequently initiated investigations of AstraZeneca, Sanofi, Novartis, Pfizer, and Eli Lilly operating in the country.

The underlying business model of pharmaceutical corruption in China involved paying physicians directly for prescriptions — a practice that exploited low base salaries in China's public hospital system and had been an open industry secret for years. GSK had implemented a global anti-bribery policy but had failed to apply adequate controls to its China operations, and local management had substantial autonomy in structuring commercial relationships.

---

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

The core failure was structural, not communicative: GSK's global compliance and anti-corruption infrastructure failed to prevent or detect a systemic, multi-year bribery operation in its largest emerging market. Whatever controls existed were not functioning. This is the baseline against which all communications responses must be measured — GSK did not get ahead of the crisis, it was caught.

GSK's initial response in the first days of the July 2013 announcement was cautious and lawyerly, acknowledging an investigation while expressing confidence in its employees and processes. This initial hedge undercut the candor that CEO Andrew Witty subsequently demonstrated and created a period of inconsistency in messaging. The company did not immediately acknowledge the likelihood of systemic wrongdoing, which allowed Chinese state media to fill the narrative gap with its own framing.

The multi-country spread of the investigation also exposed a significant weakness: GSK's global anti-corruption communications were reactive rather than coordinated. Each country investigation was handled semi-independently, creating a pattern of drip-drip disclosures that extended the story over months and maintained negative coverage.

### What Partially Worked

CEO Andrew Witty's response, once the full scale of the conduct became undeniable, was notably candid for a major multinational facing a criminal investigation. Witty publicly acknowledged that the conduct described was "shameful" and represented a "betrayal of the values of GSK." He apologized directly to the Chinese government and to patients. Crucially, he committed GSK to cooperating with Chinese authorities — and followed through. Chinese state media and government officials explicitly praised GSK's cooperation, which almost certainly moderated the eventual penalty and enabled a resolution that preserved GSK's license to operate in China.

Witty also announced substantive structural reforms: GSK committed to decoupling sales force compensation from individual prescription volumes, ending speaker fees to physicians as a marketing tool, and overhauling its China compliance infrastructure. These were genuine operational changes, not cosmetic commitments, and they were reported as such. Several other multinational pharmaceutical companies subsequently adopted similar compensation reforms.

## Outcome & Legacy

- GSK paid RMB 3 billion ($490 million) to Chinese authorities — the largest foreign company criminal fine in China at the time.
- Five GSK China executives convicted; China general manager received a suspended sentence and was deported.
- GSK retained its operating license in China and continued to operate and grow its China business.
- The case accelerated industry-wide reform of pharmaceutical sales practices in China; multiple multinationals restructured physician engagement models.
- The Chinese government's use of GSK as a public example triggered parallel investigations of at least five other major pharmaceutical companies.
- CEO Andrew Witty's communications response is now cited in business school case studies as a model of how candor and cooperation can limit reputational damage — though only in the context of genuine and substantive wrongdoing having occurred.
- The case significantly elevated anti-corruption compliance as a strategic priority for multinational pharmaceutical companies operating in emerging markets.

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Candor after being caught is not the same as proactive ethics — but it is meaningfully better than denial.** Witty's acknowledgment of "shameful" conduct limited damage because it was credible. A denial strategy would have been challenged by the volume of evidence already in Chinese government hands.
2. **Cooperation with investigating authorities in non-Western jurisdictions can be more effective than legal resistance.** Chinese authorities explicitly credited GSK's cooperation in public statements. This is not a universally applicable principle — it requires careful legal analysis — but the reputational outcome is instructive.
3. **Structural reforms announced during a crisis must be genuine to be effective.** GSK's commitment to reform physician compensation was specific, measurable, and verifiable. Vague commitments to "review our processes" would not have provided the same communications anchor.
4. **Multi-country corruption crises require centralized communications coordination.** The drip-disclosure problem — each new country investigation extending the story — is preventable with a centralized crisis communications protocol that anticipates the full scope before disclosures happen.
5. **Industry-wide practices do not constitute a defense.** GSK could not credibly argue that its conduct was unusual in the industry. The communications response had to focus on what GSK was doing differently going forward, not on contextualizing past conduct.
6. **The CEO must be the face of accountability.** Witty's visibility was essential to the response's credibility. Had GSK communicated through spokespersons or legal statements while the CEO remained absent, Chinese government officials and media would have interpreted it as evasion.
7. **Anti-corruption compliance failures in emerging markets are a communications crisis waiting to happen.** The bribery model in China was institutionalized. Pharmaceutical companies with similar commercial models in other markets should treat this case as a prediction, not just a warning.
