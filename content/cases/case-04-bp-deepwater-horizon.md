## The Crisis

On April 20, 2010, the Deepwater Horizon drilling rig — operated by BP 50 miles off the Louisiana coast — suffered a catastrophic blowout. The explosion killed 11 workers and injured 17 others. The wellhead 1,500 metres below the Gulf of Mexico could not be capped. For 87 days, an estimated 4.9 million barrels of crude oil — the largest marine oil spill in US history — poured into the Gulf. The oil devastated beaches, wetlands, coral reefs, and marine ecosystems across five US states. The disaster unfolded on live international television, with underwater cameras streaming the uncontrolled gusher 24 hours a day.

BP had been under regulatory pressure before the disaster for safety violations at other facilities. The Macondo well had experienced warning signs — a negative pressure test result was misread on the morning of the explosion. The blowout preventer, the last line of defence, failed.

## PR & Communications Response

### Structural Failures Before the Crisis

BP had cut its PR and crisis management budgets in the years preceding the disaster. There was no tested crisis communications plan, no pre-designated spokesperson, and no rapid response infrastructure in place for an event of this scale. Critically, BP had also reduced its government relations investment — the relationships needed to manage a crisis of this political magnitude did not exist.

### The Tony Hayward Problem

CEO Tony Hayward became the defining symbol of BP's communications failure. His statements form a textbook of what not to say during a crisis:

- **"The Gulf of Mexico is a very big ocean. The amount of oil and dispersant we are putting into it is tiny in relation to the total water volume."** (Minimisation framing — contradicted by live footage of the spreading slick)
- **"I think the environmental impact of this disaster is likely to be very, very modest."** (False prediction that the visible evidence contradicted daily)
- **"There's no one who wants this thing over more than I do. You know, I'd like my life back."** (Self-centered statement made while the families of 11 dead workers were still grieving — became the most-quoted crisis communication failure of the decade)
- Hayward attended a yacht race off the Isle of Wight while the spill continued — photographs of him on his yacht with the caption "What Tony did on his day off" circulated globally

### Inconsistent Technical Estimates

BP's early estimates of the spill rate were dramatically understated. The company initially said 1,000 barrels per day were leaking; independent scientists estimated 12,000–19,000; the eventual figure was approximately 62,000 barrels per day at peak. Each upward revision made BP's previous statements look like deliberate deception.

### The Live Camera Problem

BP was required by the US government to stream live underwater footage of the wellhead. This created an unprecedented crisis communications dynamic: the public could watch the company's failure in real time, 24 hours a day, on any device. No spokesperson statement, no press release, no ad campaign could compete with the live visual of oil pouring uncontrolled into the Gulf. BP had no communications strategy for this situation.

### The "$20 Billion Escrow Fund" — One Thing That Worked

Under White House pressure, BP established a $20 billion independently administered claims fund within two months of the disaster. This was a communications win: it demonstrated scale of commitment, removed BP from the role of sole arbiter of compensation, and gave affected communities a concrete, visible mechanism for recovery. It limited (though did not prevent) some of the worst political and legal escalation.

### BP's Advertising Campaign

BP ran a substantial advertising campaign featuring cleanup workers and Gulf communities, with messaging about BP's commitment to restoration. The campaign was criticised for spending money on marketing rather than cleanup, and for featuring workers who were reportedly told to pose for cameras. The contrast between the advertising imagery and the ongoing visible disaster made the campaign counterproductive.

## Outcome & Legacy

- **$65B+** total cost in cleanup, fines, and legal settlements — the most expensive corporate environmental settlement in history
- BP's US market capitalisation fell approximately **$90 billion** in the months following the explosion
- Tony Hayward was removed as CEO in July 2010
- BP pleaded guilty to 11 counts of felony manslaughter, two misdemeanours, and a felony count of lying to Congress
- The disaster triggered a comprehensive overhaul of US offshore drilling safety regulations
- Deepwater Horizon is now the defining global reference for oil spill crisis communications failure alongside Exxon Valdez

---

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Cutting crisis management infrastructure creates catastrophic liability.** The investment in crisis plans, trained spokespersons, and government relationships is insurance — its absence is visible only when the crisis arrives.
2. **One CEO gaffe can override months of communications effort.** Hayward's "I'd like my life back" statement defined BP's response more completely than any press release, ad, or remediation update.
3. **Live visual media eliminates narrative control.** When the disaster is visually uncontrolled and continuously broadcast, no communications strategy can manage public perception — only stopping the disaster can.
4. **Estimate conservatively in public and revise upward once.** Repeated upward revisions of the spill rate created a compounding credibility collapse. A single, honest estimate — even if uncertain — is more manageable than serial revisions.
5. **Independently administered remediation funds are more credible than company-controlled ones.** The $20B escrow fund worked precisely because BP was not administering it.
6. **CEO symbolic behaviour is part of crisis communications.** A yacht race while 11 families are grieving and millions of gallons of oil fill the Gulf is not a private decision.

## Latin America Application

| BP Mistake | Latin America Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| No crisis communications infrastructure | Remote LatAm operations (Amazonian, offshore, Andean) face even more constrained response capacity than the Gulf of Mexico | Pre-position crisis response infrastructure in-country; test before operations begin |
| CEO self-referential statements | Executive media training is rarely prioritised in LatAm market entry plans | Mandatory spokesperson training in Spanish/Portuguese before operations; pre-approved holding statements |
| Serial upward revision of damage estimates | In LatAm political environments, repeated revisions trigger legislative investigations and license reviews | Establish independent environmental monitoring from Day 1; let third-party data be the source of public figures |
| Advertising during active disaster | Community trust in LatAm energy markets is already scarred by Chevron/Texaco, Petrobras — any perception of image management over substance deepens distrust | Suspend brand advertising during active crises; communicate only operational updates and community support actions |
