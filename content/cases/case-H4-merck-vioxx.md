## The Crisis

Vioxx (rofecoxib) was a COX-2 inhibitor painkiller developed by Merck and approved by the FDA in 1999 for arthritis and acute pain. It was positioned as a safer alternative to traditional NSAIDs because it caused fewer gastrointestinal side effects. Merck marketed Vioxx aggressively, reaching 50 million patients in over 80 countries before its withdrawal. Peak annual sales reached $2.5 billion.

Internal documents later revealed that Merck scientists had raised concerns about cardiovascular risk as early as 1997 — before the drug was approved. The critical turning point came with the VIGOR trial in 2000, which showed that patients taking Vioxx had five times the rate of heart attacks compared to those taking naproxen. Merck's public response was not to acknowledge a Vioxx risk but to argue that naproxen had a cardioprotective effect — a hypothesis that was speculative and not supported by evidence. This framing allowed Merck to continue marketing while deflecting a legitimate safety signal.

On September 30, 2004, Merck voluntarily withdrew Vioxx after the APPROVE trial — a colorectal polyp prevention study — showed that 18 or more months of use doubled the risk of heart attack and stroke. The withdrawal itself was handled in a textbook manner: it was proactive, CEO-led, and accompanied by a clear public statement. FDA Commissioner Lester Crawford initially praised Merck's decision to act voluntarily. CEO Raymond Gilmartin held a press conference the same day, stating Merck was "acting with an abundance of caution."

However, FDA internal safety analyst Dr. David Graham told a Senate Finance Committee hearing in November 2004 that between 88,000 and 139,000 Americans had suffered heart attacks attributable to Vioxx, with roughly 30–40% of those proving fatal. The picture that emerged was not of a company that had acted responsibly upon discovering a risk, but of a company that had known about the risk for years, suppressed internal dissent, and aggressively marketed a dangerous drug. Total litigation settlements ultimately reached approximately $4.85 billion.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

The fundamental failure was not the September 2004 withdrawal announcement — it was the five years of deliberate crisis avoidance that preceded it. Merck's scientific and communications teams actively promoted the "naproxen hypothesis" to explain the VIGOR trial results, a strategy that allowed the company to shift public and regulatory focus away from a real safety signal. This was not ambiguity or scientific uncertainty; internal emails showed that senior Merck scientists discussed how to avoid the cardiovascular issue in communications with physicians.

Merck trained its sales force to use a "dodge and defer" card — a laminated reference document — to deflect cardiologists' questions about Vioxx's heart attack risk. When outside scientists published papers raising safety concerns, Merck's communications team drafted response strategies to discredit the authors. Dr. Eric Topol of the Cleveland Clinic, who published an early warning about Vioxx in 2001, described coordinated attempts by Merck to undermine his findings.

The 2004 withdrawal announcement, though well-executed on the day, was therefore structurally undermined: the CEO who stepped forward to "do the right thing" was the same CEO who had approved the years of deflection. Media coverage that initially praised Merck's transparency shifted sharply as internal documents entered public view through litigation discovery.

### What Partially Worked

The September 30 withdrawal announcement itself followed many best practices. Merck acted voluntarily before the FDA compelled action. CEO Gilmartin was visible and available. The company distributed consistent messaging to physicians, patients, and media simultaneously. Merck established a dedicated patient response line. In the immediate 48-hour window, the company received significant credit for transparency — a credit that eroded as the full history became clear.

## Outcome & Legacy

- Merck paid approximately $4.85 billion to settle approximately 27,000 personal injury lawsuits.
- Vioxx was never re-approved; the entire COX-2 inhibitor class faced heightened scrutiny. Bextra (valdecoxib) was also withdrawn.
- Raymond Gilmartin resigned as CEO in 2005.
- The case prompted significant FDA reform discussions and contributed to the FDA Amendments Act of 2007, which strengthened post-market safety surveillance authority.
- Dr. David Graham's Senate testimony became a landmark in whistleblower advocacy within pharmaceutical regulation.
- The case is now standard curriculum in pharmaceutical ethics, FDA regulatory law, and crisis communications programs globally.
- Merck's litigation settlement structure — a rare single-payout resolution — became a model for pharmaceutical mass tort management.

## Key PR Lessons

1. **A good withdrawal does not undo a bad pattern.** A single well-executed press conference cannot compensate for years of documented concealment. The quality of a crisis response is evaluated in the context of the full history, not just the day of announcement.
2. **The "scientific uncertainty" defense has a documentary expiration date.** If internal records show decision-makers knew a risk was real and chose to frame it as uncertain, those records will eventually surface in litigation — and they will be devastating.
3. **Training sales forces to deflect creates corporate liability.** The "dodge and defer" card was not just an ethical failure; it was a legally traceable artifact of institutional intent to suppress safety information.
4. **Attacking credible external scientists creates additional adversaries.** Merck's response to Dr. Topol's published concerns turned an academic warning into a personal conflict that amplified media coverage and motivated further scrutiny.
5. **CEO visibility must be matched by CEO accountability.** Gilmartin's press conference on September 30 was praised precisely because it was CEO-led — but that same visibility made his eventual resignation and the documentation of prior knowledge far more damaging to the company.
6. **The 72-hour protocol applies to safety signals, not just public crises.** The VIGOR trial result in 2000 was the actual crisis trigger. The 2004 withdrawal was the consequence of failing to apply crisis protocol four years earlier.
7. **Regulators and external analysts are not adversaries to manage — they are validation sources to engage.** Had Merck worked transparently with FDA after VIGOR, the damage would have been far smaller. The adversarial relationship with regulators extended the crisis and expanded liability.

## Latin America Application

| Mistake/Lesson | LATAM Risk | MINERBA Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Suppressing safety signals while continuing active marketing | Vioxx was widely prescribed across Latin America for 5 years; patients in markets with weaker pharmacovigilance systems had no independent source of safety alerts | Counsel clients that pharmacovigilance obligations extend to all markets; asymmetric safety disclosures across geographies are legally and reputationally indefensible |
| Using "scientific uncertainty" framing to delay action | Regulatory agencies in many LATAM countries have fewer resources for independent post-market surveillance and may rely on the manufacturer's own framing of uncertain data | Build a communications protocol that defaults to disclosure when a plausible signal exists, regardless of local regulatory requirements |
| Training sales forces to deflect physician questions | Medical sales relationships in LATAM healthcare systems are often more direct and personal; scripted deflection is culturally visible and likely to generate internal whistleblowers | Sales force communications must be consistent with scientific affairs and regulatory positions; any divergence creates risk |
| Slow response to external scientific criticism | Academic and NGO voices in LATAM (e.g., Pan American Health Organization, national pharmacology institutes) carry strong credibility with health ministries | Engage external scientific critics directly and early; dismissal of academic criticism in LATAM contexts tends to attract government scrutiny |
| Withdrawal timing tied to legal exposure rather than patient safety | LATAM withdrawal timing, if delayed relative to the U.S., will be interpreted as a deliberate two-tier safety standard — an extreme reputational risk | Simultaneous multi-market withdrawal protocols must be built into product safety crisis plans |
