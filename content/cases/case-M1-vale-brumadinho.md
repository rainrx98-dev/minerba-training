## The Crisis

At approximately 12:28 PM on January 25, 2019, the Córrego do Feijão tailings dam — owned entirely by Vale S.A. — collapsed near the city of Brumadinho, Minas Gerais, Brazil. A wall of toxic mining waste engulfed Vale's own administrative buildings, worker canteens, and the surrounding community. **270 people died**, including Vale employees eating lunch in the company cafeteria directly below the dam. It was the deadliest industrial disaster in Brazil's history.

The dam had been inactive — no longer receiving tailings — since 2015. Brazilian regulations required inactive dams to be decommissioned. Vale had received multiple extensions on decommissioning deadlines. The dam was rated stable by the German engineering firm TÜV SÜD just seven months before it collapsed.

Three years earlier, on November 5, 2015, Vale had been a 50% owner (alongside BHP Billiton) of Samarco, whose Fundão dam collapsed near Mariana, MG, killing 19 people and releasing toxic mud 650 km down the Rio Doce to the Atlantic. Brumadinho happened with that failure fresh in institutional memory — and fresh in the public's.

---

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

**CEO Appeared to Minimize and Deflect — Immediately:** CEO Fabio Schvartsman, in his first major public statement, criticized TÜV SÜD and appeared to shift blame to the German inspectors for certifying the dam as stable. The statement landed as deflection while bodies were still being recovered. Schvartsman had previously, after the Samarco disaster, said: *"We had Samarco, and it was a unique fact. There can be no more Samarcos."* Those words were now everywhere.

**No Empathy Architecture:** Vale's initial communications were process-oriented — statements about investigations, geological factors, and stability certifications. There was no visible, sustained CEO expression of grief for the dead. At a scale of 270 fatalities, including Vale's own workers, the absence of sustained human acknowledgment was devastating.

**The "Isolated Incident" Framing Destroyed Credibility:** Given Samarco/Mariana in 2015, Vale could not credibly frame Brumadinho as an unforeseeable event. Journalists immediately published timelines showing two catastrophic tailings dam failures by the same controlling company within 40 months. The framing of "unforeseen geological event" was unsustainable.

**Governance Defense:** The company pointed to independent certification, regulatory compliance, and its track record on active dams. This legal-posture response during an acute human tragedy destroyed goodwill. Audiences understood that 270 people were dead while the company's first instinct was to protect its liability position.

**CEO Departure Under Pressure:** Schvartsman eventually stepped down in March 2019, approximately six weeks after the collapse, under sustained pressure from Brazilian prosecutors, institutional shareholders, and the government. The delayed resignation meant he remained the public face of the crisis for its most intense period — without the credibility to lead it.

### What Was Done Better (Relative to 2015)

Vale did move faster on victim compensation and rehabilitation commitments than in the Samarco/Renova Foundation process. The company publicly committed to decommissioning all upstream-construction dams globally — a substantive, visible structural commitment. These actions, however, were partially overshadowed by the deflection in the first 72 hours.

---

## What a Better Response Would Have Looked Like

**Within 2 hours:** CEO on camera — not at headquarters, physically moving toward Brumadinho — expressing unconditional grief. No mention of TÜV SÜD. No mention of geological factors. One message: *"Our people are dead. We are responsible. We are doing everything to find them."*

**Within 24 hours:** Full suspension of all upstream-construction dams globally, announced proactively (not reactively). Independent investigation announced with terms of reference set by the government, not Vale.

**Within 72 hours:** Commitment to individual victim family support — not routed through a foundation but directly administered, with a named executive responsible and a public contact number.

**The structural lesson:** When a company has previously suffered a near-identical crisis (Samarco 2015), any reoccurrence must be treated as a governance failure — not an operational accident. Communications must lead with accountability for systemic failure, not with explanation of the immediate technical cause.

## What Actually Happened

**Legal:** In January 2021, Brazilian authorities charged Schvartsman and 15 others — including TÜV SÜD engineers — with homicide and environmental crimes. In 2021, Vale reached an initial R$37.7 billion (~$7B USD) reparations agreement with Minas Gerais state, the largest corporate settlement in Brazilian history at the time. Criminal proceedings continued into 2024.

**Reputational:** Vale's share price fell approximately 25% in the days following the collapse, erasing approximately $19 billion in market capitalization. The company's ESG ratings were downgraded by major index providers.

**Structural:** Vale committed to decommissioning 30 upstream-construction dams globally. As of 2023, progress has been made but several dams remain in transition, under continued regulatory scrutiny.

---

## Lessons for Minerba Consultants

1. **Repeat failures require a different playbook.** When a company has survived a similar prior crisis, the default is cynicism — not benefit of the doubt. Communications must actively acknowledge the pattern, not just the incident.

2. **Technical deflection during human tragedy is always wrong.** Inspectors, certifications, and geological analysis belong in engineering reports, not in the CEO's first public statement when people are being pulled from mud.

3. **In Brazil and Latin America, worker and community fatalities carry political weight.** The governor, federal prosecutors, and the Senate mobilized within hours. Crisis communications must anticipate political escalation, not treat it as secondary.

4. **Social license, once destroyed twice, is nearly impossible to fully recover.** The cumulative effect of Samarco + Brumadinho defined Vale's reputation globally for years. This is the cost of insufficiently acting on lessons from the first crisis.
