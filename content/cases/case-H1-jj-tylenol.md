## Why This Case Is Different

This is the only case in MINERBA's library with a **"Effective Response"** PR rating. It is taught not as a warning but as a model. The crisis itself was severe — seven people died — but the company's response transformed a potential brand-ending catastrophe into a legacy of consumer trust that defined the gold standard for crisis communications for four decades.

The key paradox: **J&J bore no legal responsibility.** A criminal third party committed the poisonings. The company could have defended its position, limited the recall to Chicago, and waited for law enforcement to resolve it. Instead, it acted as if it bore full moral responsibility — and that decision is why Tylenol is still the leading OTC analgesic in the US today.

---

## The Crisis

Beginning on **September 29, 1982**, seven people died in the Chicago metropolitan area after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules that had been laced with potassium cyanide. An unknown perpetrator had removed Tylenol bottles from store shelves, added cyanide to capsules, and returned them to stores. The tampering occurred after manufacture — J&J's production processes were not involved.

The seven victims:

- Mary Kellerman, 12, Elk Grove Village
- Adam Janus, 27, Arlington Heights
- Stanley Janus, 25, Lisle (Adam's brother)
- Theresa Janus, 19 (Stanley's wife)
- Mary Reiner, 27, Winfield
- Paula Prince, 35, Chicago
- Mary McFarland, 31, Elmhurst

The FBI and FDA confirmed no tampering occurred at manufacturing. The perpetrator was never identified or charged. The crimes remain officially unsolved.

Before the crisis, Tylenol held approximately **35% of the US over-the-counter analgesic market** — the dominant brand in a high-margin, highly competitive category.

---

## The Response — What J&J Did

### CEO James Burke's Decision Framework

Chairman and CEO **James E. Burke** led the response personally. He convened a seven-person crisis management committee within hours of the first reports. The first decision point: the FBI and some FDA advisors recommended a regional (Chicago-area only) recall, arguing a national recall would cause unnecessary panic and might encourage copycat crimes.

Burke's response was guided by J&J's corporate **Credo** — a values document written by Chairman Robert Wood Johnson in 1943 that explicitly placed the company's responsibility to "customers and the communities in which we live and work" above its responsibility to "stockholders." Burke had famously convened a Credo challenge in the late 1970s — asking executives whether the document was still meant seriously or was merely decorative. The consensus was: it was meant seriously.

The Credo gave Burke a framework that could be explained publicly and that provided non-negotiable guidance when financial and legal pressures pointed in the opposite direction.

### What J&J Did

**1. National recall within days:** J&J issued a voluntary national recall of all Tylenol capsule products — approximately **31 million bottles** with a retail value of approximately **$100 million**. This was broader than what the FBI or FDA requested. No other state had reported contaminated products. The company recalled nationally anyway.

**2. Consumer warning:** J&J immediately issued nationwide advisories telling consumers not to take Tylenol capsules until further notice.

**3. Production and advertising halt:** All Tylenol advertising was suspended. Production was halted until new packaging could be developed.

**4. CEO on television:** Burke appeared publicly and repeatedly, including on CBS News's 60 Minutes, in a widely noted interview that demonstrated visible accountability and genuine empathy without deflection.

**5. Cooperation with authorities:** J&J cooperated fully and proactively with the FDA, FBI, and Chicago police, providing all company records and access.

**6. Tamper-resistant packaging innovation:** J&J developed and introduced a new triple-seal tamper-resistant capsule packaging — foil seal under the cap, shrink-wrap band around the neck, redesigned sealed capsules — and reintroduced Tylenol in this new format in **November 1982**, approximately six weeks after the crisis began.

The new packaging solved the safety problem structurally. It also gave media a positive story to cover alongside the tragedy: J&J had made every medicine safer. The FDA subsequently required tamper-resistant packaging for all over-the-counter medicines, making J&J's response a public safety legacy.

## The Recovery

| Metric | Before Crisis | Immediately After | One Year Later |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market share | ~35% | ~7–8% | ~30% |
| Brand perception | Strong | Severely damaged | Restored |
| Industry standard | No tamper sealing | — | Tamper-resistant required by FDA |

Tylenol did not merely recover — it eventually returned to and exceeded its pre-crisis market share, remaining the US market leader in OTC analgesics for decades.

## Why This Worked — The PR Principles

### 1. Values-Led Decision Making Over Financial Calculation

The recall cost approximately $100 million. J&J made the decision before financial analysis was complete, anchored on the Credo. This made the decision legible to media and consumers: the company wasn't calculating, it was choosing.

### 2. Speed Before Completeness

J&J acted on incomplete information. Not all facts were known. The geographic extent of tampering was uncertain. Burke chose to over-respond rather than wait for certainty — the opposite of every instinct institutional lawyers give clients.

### 3. National Scope Exceeded Requirements

By doing more than the FBI asked, J&J signaled that consumer safety, not liability management, was the actual decision criterion. That signal is the core of the story.

### 4. CEO Personal Visibility and Accountability

Burke was the face of the response. He was visible, accessible, and consistent. He did not delegate to communications staff. The company's most senior decision-maker owned the crisis.

### 5. Constructive Legacy

The tamper-resistant packaging turned a tragedy into an industry improvement. Media could write about J&J's contribution to public safety, not only about seven deaths. The narrative had a second act.

### 6. Moral Ownership Without Legal Obligation

The most frequently cited lesson: J&J took moral ownership of consumer harm caused by a criminal third party. They did not wait for legal compulsion. They did not issue liability disclaimers. They acted as if the harm was theirs to fix — because it affected their customers, and their customers were what the Credo said they were responsible for.

## Lessons for Minerba Consultants

1. **A values document is only useful if the CEO is willing to defend it under financial pressure.** J&J's Credo had been discussed seriously by leadership before the crisis. When the crisis came, Burke used it as a decision framework, not as boilerplate. For Minerba clients: does the company actually have values leadership is willing to act on? Find out before the crisis.

2. **Over-responding — doing more than legally required — is the highest-credibility signal available in a crisis.** It is also the most expensive. It must be decided at the executive level, not by communications teams.

3. **Speed of moral acknowledgment matters more than speed of factual completeness.** "We don't yet know the full extent, but we are pulling every bottle off every shelf until we do" is a more credible statement than waiting six days for complete information before speaking.

4. **The second act matters.** What structural improvement does your client's response produce? The tamper-resistant packaging gave journalists and consumers a positive story to close on. Every crisis response should aim to produce a structural outcome that can be reported.

5. **In Latin American contexts, the J&J model is frequently cited by regulators and media as the benchmark.** When advising clients in the region, referencing J&J's Credo-led approach and its concrete recall scope provides a credible comparison point in press briefings and government meetings.
