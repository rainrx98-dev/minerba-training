## The Crisis

On September 18, 2015, the US Environmental Protection Agency issued a Notice of Violation to Volkswagen AG, revealing that the company had installed software — a "defeat device" — in its diesel vehicles that detected when the car was being subjected to an emissions test and temporarily altered engine performance to reduce nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions to legal levels. Under real-world driving conditions, the same vehicles emitted NOx at 10 to 40 times the legal limit. The software had been designed to pass the test and then switch back to a higher-performance, higher-emissions mode on the road.

The defeat device had been present in VW's diesel vehicles since at least 2009. Internal evidence later showed that Bosch, the parts supplier that provided the emissions control hardware, had warned Volkswagen in 2007 that using the defeat device mode on public roads would be illegal. VW proceeded anyway. The decision was made at a sufficient level of seniority within the company that subsequent investigations concluded this was not the unauthorized action of rogue engineers but a deliberate corporate strategy to allow VW's "clean diesel" marketing claims — a centerpiece of the company's US growth strategy — to be maintained while meeting performance targets that were incompatible with genuine emissions compliance.

The initial scope of the violation was described as affecting approximately 500,000 vehicles in the United States. Within days, VW was forced to acknowledge that the defeat device had been installed in approximately 11 million diesel vehicles globally, across multiple brands in the Volkswagen Group including Volkswagen, Audi, Porsche, Skoda, and SEAT. The financial exposure grew correspondingly: VW initially set aside €6.5 billion, a figure that would ultimately rise to more than €30 billion in total costs including fines, settlements, vehicle buybacks, and legal fees.

The US resolution alone was devastating: a $4.3 billion criminal plea with the US Department of Justice, a $14.7 billion civil settlement covering consumer restitution and environmental remediation, and the indictment of multiple VW executives including the head of the company's US environmental compliance unit. Several executives were arrested or had their passports seized. The company's CEO Martin Winterkorn resigned on September 23 — four days after the EPA notice — but not before issuing a statement that was widely criticized as inadequate. VW's global reputation for engineering integrity, built over decades, was severely and durably damaged.

## PR & Communications Response

### What Failed

Martin Winterkorn's initial statement expressed personal "shock" at the revelations but was evasive about what management knew and when. The decision to have Winterkorn resign four days after the EPA notice — rather than immediately — meant four days of damaging follow-up coverage asking why he was still in position. His resignation statement did not acknowledge the fraudulent intent of the defeat device, instead framing his departure as a personal decision to allow the company to rebuild trust — a formulation that further angered media and regulators who wanted accountability, not voluntary succession.

VW's communications teams in Germany and the United States were visibly misaligned in the early weeks. German-language communications were more defensive and legally cautious; US-facing communications were slow and appeared to under-brief American spokespeople on the full technical scope of the problem. The company's initial characterization of the issue as a "discrepancy" in emissions data was immediately undermined when the EPA clarified that this was a deliberately programmed fraud. The word "discrepancy" became a symbol of corporate evasion and was widely cited as an example of crisis communications failure.

VW also badly managed the disclosure of scope. Learning progressively — 500,000 vehicles, then 11 million — that the problem was larger than initially acknowledged created serial "second-day stories" that extended the acute media phase and reinforced the impression that the company was not being forthcoming. A controlled, comprehensive disclosure of the full 11 million figure in the first 72 hours would have been far less damaging than the drip-feed that occurred.

### What Partially Worked

VW did move quickly in the United States to engage legal counsel and enter formal settlement negotiations with the DOJ and the EPA, which ultimately limited criminal exposure for the corporate entity and produced a settlement structure that, while enormously costly, gave the company a defined path forward. The appointment of Matthias Muller as CEO brought a clean face to the recovery narrative. VW's long-run communications recovery — emphasizing its pivot to electric vehicles and its Dieselgate accountability — was handled more competently than the acute-phase response, and the company has substantially rebuilt its global market position. The lesson is that recovery is possible from even severe fraud crises, but the cost is dramatically higher when the acute response fails.

## Outcome & Legacy

- Total costs exceeded €30 billion globally, making Dieselgate the most expensive automotive scandal in history
- The US criminal plea included the indictment of six VW executives and the appointment of an independent compliance monitor
- VW's "clean diesel" marketing strategy was permanently discredited; the broader diesel passenger car market in Europe collapsed
- The scandal accelerated VW's pivot to electric vehicles — the company announced its ID. series and a major EV investment program in the aftermath
- Regulatory consequences spread globally: the EU, South Korea, Canada, Australia, and other jurisdictions opened investigations and imposed penalties
- The case became a definitive illustration of how "greenwashing" — claiming environmental credentials that products do not possess — creates fraud exposure under securities and consumer protection law
- By 2022, VW had substantially rebuilt its market position and was one of the largest EV manufacturers globally, demonstrating that recovery from major fraud crises is achievable but enormously costly

## Key PR Lessons

1. **Disclose the full scope in the first 72 hours.** The drip-feed from 500,000 to 11 million vehicles extended the acute crisis phase and destroyed credibility. A complete and accurate disclosure on Day 1, however painful, limits the media lifecycle of the crisis.
2. **"Discrepancy" and similar minimizing language always backfires.** When a regulator has used the word "fraud," a corporate response that uses softer language reads as evasion and generates its own news cycle.
3. **CEO transitions must be clean and immediate.** A four-day delay in Winterkorn's resignation turned the resignation into a second crisis rather than a reset. When the CEO's position is untenable, the decision should be made in hours, not days.
4. **Cross-border communications require a single coordinated voice.** The misalignment between VW's German and US communications created contradictory messages that each market could report as evidence of inconsistency or deception.
5. **Legal compliance framing is insufficient when the underlying conduct is fraudulent.** "We are cooperating with regulators" is not a communications strategy when the company has admitted to deliberately programming vehicles to cheat regulatory tests.
6. **Defeat device-style deceptions are detectable.** The emissions discrepancy was identified by an academic research team (West Virginia University) commissioned by the International Council on Clean Transportation. Technical deceptions designed to fool regulators will eventually be exposed by independent third parties with access to real-world data.
7. **Recovery is possible, but the cost of acute-phase failure is enormous.** VW's post-crisis pivot to EVs demonstrates that companies can rebuild from severe fraud crises — but the difference between a well-managed and a poorly managed acute response is measured in tens of billions of dollars and years of lost credibility.
